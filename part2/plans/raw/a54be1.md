## The question, sharpened

The loose version of the question — "does portal MAU predict ARR?" — is unanswerable as stated, because it bundles three separate claims: that a third-party panel measures real logins, that real logins move with the number of seats a customer is contractually paying for, and that seats move with ARR. Only the middle claim is interesting and only the middle claim is in doubt. The first is a measurement problem with a known solution; the third is an accounting identity plus a price/mix residual. So the project is organized around answering the middle claim first, in isolation, on the subset of companies where the answer is checkable, and refusing to build anything downstream if the answer is no.

**Primary hypothesis (the gate).** For seat-based SaaS companies, year-over-year growth in panel-measured monthly active users of the client-facing application is a contemporaneous, unbiased-up-to-scale proxy for year-over-year growth in billable seats. Formally: in a pooled within-company regression of Δ₄ln(seats) on Δ₄ln(panel MAU), the slope is materially positive, the within-company correlation is at least moderate, and the relationship is stable across time and across firms rather than driven by two or three names.

**Secondary hypothesis (conditional on the gate).** Because ARR ≡ seats × revenue-per-seat, and because MAU can only speak to the seat term, a MAU-based nowcast of ARR growth should beat a naive persistence forecast by a margin roughly proportional to the share of ARR growth variance attributable to seats. If seats explain 60% of ARR growth variance and MAU tracks seats with R² of 0.5, the honest ceiling on ARR-growth R² is around 0.3, and any backtest that claims more is almost certainly leaking information.

**Kill criterion, written before any modeling.** If the gate fails on the disclosure subset, the project stops and the deliverable is a negative result plus a taxonomy of *why* it failed (free tiers, shelfware, deskless seats, panel coverage). We do not proceed to "well, MAU still correlates with revenue" — that path is where every spurious result in this literature comes from.

## Universe and the disclosure subset

Start from US- and Israel-listed SaaS companies with an identifiable per-seat or per-user list price and a public web application at a distinguishable host (`app.*`, `*.my.*`, a login subdomain). Exclude anything whose primary meter is consumption (Snowflake, Datadog, Twilio, MongoDB Atlas, Confluent), anything whose value is transacted rather than logged into (payments, marketplaces), and anything where the portal is used by the customer's *customers* rather than by billable users.

The gate sample is the subset that discloses a seat-like count with quarterly or at least annual frequency. Candidates worth chasing, in rough order of data quality:

- **Payroll and HCM**, which is the richest vein because the billable unit is literally a headcount that gets disclosed: ADP's pays-per-control, Paycom's and Paylocity's clients plus average employees per client, Dayforce's live customers and employees on platform. These give a near-ground-truth seat series.
- **Collaboration and productivity** with paying-user disclosure: Dropbox paying users, Atlassian's customer count plus disclosed seat-tier mix, Asana's and monday.com's customers above spend thresholds, Smartsheet's disclosed average ACV per domain-based customer.
- **Companies that disclosed seats in an S-1 and stopped**, which are usable for the historical window and are also the single most important source of selection bias (see pitfalls).
- **Private-market and channel sources**: reseller price lists, Microsoft/Google marketplace tier data, and G2/Gartner deployment-size fields, used only as sanity checks, never as regression targets.

Realistically this yields 12–25 companies and 8–20 quarters each — call it 150–400 firm-quarters. That is small. Every design decision below is made with that N in mind: pooling, shrinkage, few parameters, and a bias toward tests that are honest at small samples rather than tests that look sophisticated.

## Data required

**Panel MAU.** One primary vendor and one independent replication vendor, chosen from clickstream/panel providers (Similarweb, Comscore, M Science, Yipit-style panels) plus mobile-side data (Sensor Tower, data.ai) where a mobile client matters. Required fields per company-month: unique visitors and unique devices to the application host, sessions, and — critically — the ability to restrict to *authenticated* surfaces. Marketing-site traffic is a demand signal, not a seat signal, and mixing them is the fastest way to a result that evaporates. Operationalize this as: include paths under the app host that appear only post-login (dashboard, workspace, inbox, `/a/`, `/w/`, project routes), exclude `/pricing`, `/login`, `/signup`, docs and status subdomains.

**Point-in-time panel vintages.** Store every monthly pull with the date it was pulled. Panel vendors re-weight and restate history; a backtest run on today's restated series is not a backtest. If the vendor cannot supply vintages, build them going forward and treat the historical portion as in-sample exploration only, clearly labeled.

**Company fundamentals.** From filings and shareholder letters: ARR or the closest disclosed analogue (cRPO, subscription revenue, billings), seat/user/employee counts, customer counts, disclosed net revenue retention, disclosed pricing changes and tier launches, fiscal calendar, FX exposure by geography. Capture the *as-reported* value and the reporting date, not the restated value.

**Consensus.** Sell-side consensus for revenue and, where it exists, for the seat metric, with the pre-print snapshot date. This is the benchmark that matters commercially, though the persistence baseline is the one that matters scientifically.

**Pricing/mix inputs.** Scraped list prices by tier and by quarter (Wayback Machine for history), announced price increases, and any disclosed shift in seat mix. This is what makes the decomposition real rather than a rhetorical device.

## Step 1: calibrate the panel before testing anything

Panel MAU is not MAU. Before it can be tested against seats it has to be de-noised.

Construct, per company-month, a de-duplicated unique-user series: collapse devices to users using the vendor's household/device graph where available, otherwise apply a device-to-user deflator estimated per company from the ratio of panel uniques to disclosed uniques in the quarters where both exist. Winsorize month-over-month panel changes at the 1st and 99th percentiles within company to kill obvious tagging breaks. Flag and manually inspect any month with a >25% level break, which almost always signals a domain migration, a tracking change, or a vendor methodology revision rather than a business event.

Estimate a **coverage ratio** per company-quarter: panel MAU divided by the disclosed seat or user count. Regress ln(coverage) on a linear time trend per company. If coverage drifts more than roughly 10% per year in a consistent direction, the panel is losing or gaining representativeness for that company and level-based work is dead; growth-space work survives only if the drift is smooth enough to be absorbed by a company fixed effect and a common time effect. Companies with erratic coverage (residual standard deviation of ln coverage above ~0.15) get dropped from the gate sample and noted.

Aggregate months to fiscal quarters two ways — quarter-average MAU and final-month MAU — and pre-commit to quarter-average as the primary, final-month as a robustness check, because ARR is a point-in-time measure but panel noise is high at monthly frequency and averaging is worth the small timing mismatch. Align on *fiscal* quarters, not calendar, and record the mapping explicitly; Atlassian, Salesforce-style July/January year-ends will otherwise silently misalign a third of the panel.

## Step 2: the gate test

Work entirely in year-over-year log differences. Define, for company *i* and fiscal quarter *t*:

- g^seat_{i,t} = ln(Seats_{i,t}) − ln(Seats_{i,t−4})
- g^mau_{i,t} = ln(MAU_{i,t}) − ln(MAU_{i,t−4})

Year-over-year differencing rather than quarter-over-quarter is the right choice here for three reasons: it removes the strong and idiosyncratic seasonality in both seat additions (enterprise Q4 budget flush, January headcount resets, education-calendar effects) and panel traffic (holidays, summer); it removes company fixed effects in the growth of the coverage ratio; and it matches how both management and the market actually discuss these businesses. The cost is overlapping observations and heavy serial correlation in the residual, which must be handled in inference and is handled below.

Primary specification, pooled with company fixed effects:

g^seat_{i,t} = α_i + β · g^mau_{i,t} + γ_t + ε_{i,t}

with γ_t a common time effect (or, if the sample is too thin, a single macro control such as YoY growth in aggregate US software employment or a broad tech-employment index — the point is to prevent a common hiring cycle from manufacturing correlation between two series that are both just tracking the sector). Estimate by OLS; report Driscoll–Kraay standard errors with a Newey–West lag of 4 to handle both the overlapping-window autocorrelation and cross-sectional dependence, and cross-check with a company-block bootstrap (resample whole companies, 2,000 draws) because with 15–25 clusters asymptotic cluster-robust SEs are anti-conservative.

**Pre-registered pass thresholds.** The gate passes only if all four hold:

1. β̂ is positive and statistically distinguishable from zero at the 5% level under the block bootstrap, with a bootstrap 90% interval that excludes zero.
2. β̂ falls in [0.5, 1.5]. Below 0.5 means MAU moves far more than seats and is mostly measuring engagement or free-tier churn; above 1.5 means seats move more than MAU, which implies the portal is not where the billable users are (shelfware, deskless workforce, API-driven usage). Either way the proxy is not doing what we need. Report the test of β = 1 separately — a slope near one is the strong form and licenses simple pass-through; a slope of 0.6 is still usable but requires the shrinkage to be modeled, not ignored.
3. Within-company partial correlation between g^seat and g^mau (after removing time effects) is ≥ 0.45 pooled, and is positive for at least two-thirds of individual companies. A pooled result driven by cross-sectional spread with no within-company time-series signal is worthless for nowcasting a given company's next print.
4. Stability: split the sample at the midpoint and require β̂ in the two halves to be within roughly one bootstrap standard error of each other, and require the sign to hold. Also require the result to survive leave-one-company-out — if dropping any single firm moves β̂ by more than 30% or flips significance, the finding is one company's story.

Run three diagnostics alongside, because they determine what the downstream model has to look like. First, **timing**: re-estimate with g^mau at leads and lags of one and two quarters. The economically expected pattern is that MAU *leads* seats, because usage grows inside an existing contract and is converted to paid seats at true-up or renewal; if the peak correlation is at lag one or two quarters, that is a feature and it becomes the core of the nowcast. If MAU *lags* seats, the panel is picking up onboarding after the contract, and the forecasting value is much weaker. Second, **asymmetry**: interact g^mau with an indicator for negative growth. Seat contraction is contractually sticky (customers pay for seats they've stopped using until renewal), so downside should show a flatter slope; if it does, the model needs the asymmetry or it will badly miss turning points, which is exactly when the signal would be most valuable. Third, **free-tier contamination**: for companies with a free tier, split panel traffic by any observable proxy for paid surfaces (admin console paths, enterprise SSO redirect hosts) and test whether the paid-proxy series has a higher β and correlation than total MAU. If it does, the whole pipeline should use the paid proxy.

## Step 3: decompose ARR before nowcasting it

Only if the gate passes. For every company in the broader universe, build the identity

Δ₄ln(ARR) = Δ₄ln(Seats) + Δ₄ln(ARR per seat)

where the second term is computed as a residual where seats are disclosed, and modeled where they are not. Then report, as a standalone deliverable, the variance decomposition: Var(g^ARR) = Var(g^seat) + Var(g^price) + 2·Cov. Do this pooled and per company. This number is the honest ceiling on what any usage signal can deliver, and it should be published before any model results so that the model cannot be quietly graded against a moving target.

Expect the split to differ sharply by segment: SMB-heavy, low-ACV products should be seat-dominated (seats explaining perhaps 70–85% of growth variance), while enterprise products with aggressive tier migration and annual list-price increases can have half or more of their growth in the price/mix term. Where the price/mix term dominates, say so and scope the claim down: the signal forecasts seats, not ARR, and the remaining uncertainty is a pricing question that requires a different dataset (scraped list prices, tier-mix disclosures, NRR commentary).

Watch the covariance term. A negative Cov(g^seat, g^price) is the signature of a company discounting to win volume, and it means a MAU-driven seat beat can coexist with an ARR miss. That is the single most likely way a technically correct seat call produces a losing trade, and it deserves an explicit flag in the output rather than a footnote.

## Step 4: the nowcast

Target: Δ₄ln(ARR)_{i,t} for the just-ended fiscal quarter, forecast made five business days after quarter end, when the panel's final month is available and roughly 30–40 days before the print. Produce a second forecast for t+1 as well; the near-term one is the honest test of the data's value, the forward one is where the money is.

Model, deliberately small:

Δ₄ln(ARR)_{i,t} = α_i + φ · Δ₄ln(ARR)_{i,t−1} + β₀ · g^mau_{i,t} + β₁ · g^mau_{i,t−1} + δ · Δ₄ln(price index)_{i,t} + ε_{i,t}

Four or five parameters plus fixed effects, on 150–400 observations. Estimate as a pooled panel with company fixed effects and ridge shrinkage (penalty chosen by nested cross-validation on the training folds only), then produce company-level coefficients by empirical-Bayes shrinkage of the individual-company estimates toward the pooled estimate, with shrinkage weight set by the ratio of within-company residual variance to between-company coefficient variance. This is the right structure for a short, wide panel: it lets a company with a clean twelve-quarter history earn its own beta while a company with six noisy quarters inherits the pooled one.

Include the lagged dependent variable explicitly. φ will be large — YoY growth rates in SaaS are extremely persistent, typically 0.7–0.9 at one quarter — and including it is what forces the MAU term to earn its keep against the thing it must beat.

For companies where the gate diagnostics showed asymmetry, add the negative-growth interaction. Do not add anything else. Every additional feature on this sample size is an invitation to overfit, and the whole credibility of the exercise rests on the comparison in the next section.

## Step 5: the baseline and the comparison

The benchmark is not zero and it is not "no model." Build three, in increasing order of difficulty:

1. **Persistence in growth space**: forecast Δ₄ln(ARR)_{i,t} = Δ₄ln(ARR)_{i,t−1}. This is the random walk in YoY growth and it is far harder to beat than people expect.
2. **Damped persistence**: an AR(1) in YoY growth with a pooled decay coefficient, which encodes the near-universal deceleration of SaaS growth rates and typically beats naive persistence by a few percent. This is the real baseline.
3. **Consensus**: sell-side mean, snapshotted before the print. Beating persistence is a scientific claim; beating consensus is the commercial one, and it is a much higher bar because consensus already contains management guidance.

Evaluate with strictly expanding-window walk-forward: train on all data through quarter t−1, predict t, refit, roll forward. No fold may contain any information — including panel vintages, restated fundamentals, or hyperparameter choices — that postdates the forecast date. Embargo the four quarters immediately preceding each test point when tuning any hyperparameter, because YoY overlapping windows mean adjacent observations share data.

Report RMSE in percentage points of YoY growth, MAE, and the RMSE ratio versus each baseline. Test significance with Diebold–Mariano using the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold small-sample correction and a Newey–West lag of 4, and because the model nests persistence, also report Clark–West, which corrects for the fact that the larger model is penalized for estimating parameters that are zero under the null. Cluster or block-bootstrap by company for the panel-wide statistic; a DM test that treats 300 overlapping firm-quarters as independent will find significance in noise.

**Pre-registered success threshold:** a ≥15% RMSE reduction versus damped persistence, with Clark–West p < 0.05 under the company block bootstrap, and directional accuracy on the *change* in growth rate (is growth accelerating or decelerating versus last quarter) above 60%. Anything less is reported as "not demonstrated" and the honest conclusion is that the panel is a monitoring tool, not a forecasting one.

Two further evaluations that matter more than the aggregate number. First, **leave-one-company-out**: train excluding company *i* entirely and predict it, to show the relationship generalizes to a name the model has never seen — this is the test that determines whether the framework can be applied to a new position. Second, **surprise-space**: regress the actual print's surprise versus consensus on the model's forecast minus consensus. A positive, significant slope with a hit rate above 55% on the sign is what makes this tradeable; a good RMSE with no incremental information over consensus is a model that has learned to reproduce guidance.

## Pitfalls to watch, and what to do about each

**Contracted seats versus active users.** This is the central risk, not a peripheral one. Billable seats are a contractual quantity fixed at renewal; MAU is a usage quantity that floats daily. In a downturn, usage falls immediately and seats fall only at renewal, which can be three to twelve months later. The consequence is that the relationship is asymmetric and lagged on the downside, and a model fit mostly on the expansion period will overreact to the first usage decline. Mitigation: fit and report the asymmetric specification, and never present a single β without the downside β next to it.

**Free tiers.** For a Dropbox, a Zoom, a Notion, or any freemium product, the overwhelming majority of MAU is unmonetized, and the paid and free populations can move in opposite directions. Total MAU can rise on a consumer-viral moment while paid seats shrink. Mitigation: build the paid-surface proxy in Step 2, and if it cannot be built for a company, that company is excluded rather than included with a caveat.

**Deskless and dormant seats.** Payroll, field-service, and frontline products bill for employees who log in twice a year or never. Panel MAU will systematically understate seats and, worse, its growth will be driven by the engaged minority. The coverage-ratio diagnostic catches the level problem; the correlation threshold catches the growth problem.

**Panel representativeness and enterprise blindness.** Consumer clickstream panels skew to SMB, consumer, and BYOD usage. Large-enterprise seats often sit behind managed devices, VPNs, SSO redirects and desktop clients that never touch the panel. A company that is mix-shifting toward enterprise will show decelerating MAU and accelerating ARR — a structural break that looks exactly like model failure. Mitigation: monitor the coverage-ratio trend as an early-warning indicator, and cross-check every company against a mobile/desktop-inclusive second vendor.

**Panel restatements and tagging breaks.** Vendors reweight, change device graphs, and retroactively restate. Without stored vintages the backtest is fiction. This is worth engineering effort on day one, not day thirty.

**Domain and product changes.** Rebrands, app-host migrations, acquired products moved onto a new domain, and the launch of a second product all break the series. Maintain a manually curated event log per company and treat each event as a mandatory break test.

**Disclosure endogeneity — the most insidious one.** Companies disclose seat counts when seat counts flatter them and quietly retire the metric when they don't. The gate sample is therefore biased toward firms in the phase where seats and growth are well-behaved, which biases β upward and the correlation upward. Two mitigations: include every company that *ever* disclosed for the full window in which it disclosed, including the final quarters before the metric was dropped; and explicitly test whether β differs in the last four quarters of a disclosure regime versus earlier. If it does, discount the headline gate result accordingly and say so.

**Small N and specification search.** With roughly 20 companies, the number of plausible specifications vastly exceeds the information in the data. Mitigation: write the gate thresholds, the model form, the baseline, and the success threshold into a pre-registration document before the first regression is run; keep a log of every specification tried; report the count of specifications alongside the p-value.

**ARR is not a standardized metric.** Definitions differ across companies and change within companies (annualization conventions, inclusion of services and usage overages, treatment of multi-year deals). Reconstruct a consistent definition where possible, and where a company redefines ARR mid-history, treat it as a break rather than smoothing over it.

**FX, macro, and correlated errors.** Roughly a third of ARR for most of these names is non-USD, and reported ARR often is not FX-neutral. Model in constant currency where disclosed; otherwise add an FX term. And remember that all of these companies' seat growth is partly one common variable — tech-sector headcount — which means errors are cross-sectionally correlated and any statistic that assumes independence across firms is overstated.

## Sequence and deliverables

Week one: build the universe, identify app hosts and authenticated path patterns, stand up the panel pull with vintage storage, hand-collect the disclosed seat series into a point-in-time database. Week two: panel calibration, coverage-ratio diagnostics, and the gate test with all four thresholds and three diagnostics — then a go/no-go decision written down and dated. Week three, only on a pass: the ARR decomposition and the published variance-share ceiling. Week four: the nowcast, the three baselines, walk-forward evaluation, leave-one-company-out, and surprise-space regression.

Deliverables are a gate memo (which passes, which fails, and why, with the failures categorized by mechanism), the decomposition table, the backtest with all three baseline comparisons and their significance tests, and a per-company scorecard giving the shrunken beta, the coverage-drift flag, the free-tier flag, and a plain statement of whether this company's ARR is forecastable from usage at all. The most valuable output may well be that last one: a defensible list of the five or six names where the link is strong enough to trade, and an equally defensible list of the ones where usage data is a trap.
