## The question and the claim we are actually testing

The loose version of the question — "does earnings call sentiment predict outperformance?" — is not testable as written, because sentiment is overwhelmingly a proxy for news that the market has already seen. A company that beat guidance has an upbeat call and a positive announcement return on the same day. Any naive sentiment sort will look like it works and will simply be re-discovering post-earnings-announcement drift, which has been documented since Ball and Brown (1968) and is not new information.

So the hypothesis is stated in a deliberately narrow, falsifiable form:

**H1.** Within an industry-and-size peer group, a company whose management tone on its earnings call is more positive than the tone of its peers' recent calls — after controlling for the earnings surprise, the announcement-window return, and the firm's own historical tone baseline — earns positive risk-adjusted returns over the following one to three months relative to those peers.

**H2 (the part I actually expect to survive).** The predictive content is concentrated in the unscripted Q&A segment and in management's *deviation* from its own habitual tone, not in the prepared remarks, which are lawyer-reviewed, largely templated, and recycled quarter to quarter.

**H3 (asymmetry).** Negative tone deviations predict more strongly and more persistently than positive ones, because managers have an incentive to sound constructive and a negative slip is therefore more informative.

The null we must beat: peer-relative sentiment adds nothing to a model that already contains standardized unexpected earnings, three-day announcement return, momentum, and the standard style factors.

The economic story, if H1–H3 hold: earnings calls contain soft information — hedging, evasion, unusual caution about a specific customer or geography — that is genuinely value-relevant but is not summarized in the reported numbers and is costly for the market to process at scale, so it diffuses into prices slowly. Peer-relativization matters because the entire semiconductor complex sounds anxious in a bad cycle; the question is who sounds anxious *relative to their own peers right now*.

## Data required

**Transcripts.** Ten fiscal years, 2015-01-01 through 2024-12-31, quarterly calls for the Russell 3000 constituent set as of each date. Primary source: S&P Global Market Intelligence Transcripts (formerly Capital IQ) via the Xpressfeed / Snowflake delivery — it is the cleanest for our purposes because it ships structured speaker blocks with a person ID, a role field (Executives / Analysts / Operator), a company affiliation for the analyst, and an explicit section marker separating "Presentation" from "Question and Answer". Refinitiv StreetEvents is the credible alternative and a useful cross-check on a subsample; AlphaSense/Sentieo is more convenient but harder to get as bulk history. Expect roughly 100k–130k usable calls. Budget real money and real lead time here: this licence is the long pole and should be signed in week 0, not week 3.

Critically, we need the **call timestamp to the minute** and a flag for pre-open / intraday / post-close. Transcript vendors frequently stamp the transcript *publication* time rather than the call time; we take the call start datetime from the vendor's event record, and where it is missing we fall back to the press-release timestamp in the 8-K exhibit and mark the observation as time-imputed.

**Returns and prices.** CRSP daily stock file for US names (share codes 10 and 11, exchanges NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq), with delisting returns applied — this matters because bad-tone firms are exactly the ones that delist, and dropping them manufactures a result. Daily and monthly. Add CRSP's dlret handling explicitly rather than trusting a merged file.

**Fundamentals and the surprise control.** Compustat quarterly for book equity, assets, accruals, and profitability; I/B/E/S Detail History for analyst estimates so we can compute standardized unexpected earnings from the median estimate in the 90 days before the call, using unadjusted per-share values matched to the same split basis (the I/B/E/S adjustment-factor trap is a classic silent error). Also pull the actual reported EPS from I/B/E/S rather than Compustat so numerator and denominator are on the same definition.

**Factors.** Ken French's daily and monthly research factors: Mkt-RF, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA, and the momentum factor; plus the Hou-Xue-Zhang q5 factors and the Stambaugh-Yuan mispricing factors as robustness. Also the Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity factor for the long-short series.

**Peer definition.** GICS sub-industry (8-digit) from Compustat/S&P as the primary grouping, with two alternatives carried through the whole study: (a) Hoberg-Phillips text-based fixed industry classifications (TNIC), available free from their data library and much better than SIC for firms with unusual businesses; (b) a simple 48-industry Fama-French scheme as the crude fallback. Within each industry, split by size terciles at the prior June NYSE breakpoints so that a mega-cap's tone is compared to other mega-caps.

**Costs and tradability.** Daily dollar volume and closing price from CRSP; short-borrow availability and fee from Markit/IHS Securities Finance (or, if unavailable, use the standard proxy: exclude the bottom size decile and any name with price under $5 from the short leg and report the result as an upper bound on the short side).

**Point-in-time discipline.** Every merge is keyed on a date the information was actually public. Compustat items are lagged to their filing date via the RDQ field or the SEC full-text index; index membership uses the historical constituent file, never the current one; GICS uses the historical assignment. Any variable we cannot timestamp gets dropped rather than approximated.

## Building the sentiment model

**Step 1: parse, don't scrape.** Ingest the structured transcript, retaining for every utterance: call ID, speaker ID, speaker role, speaker title string, employer, section (prepared vs Q&A), turn index, and the raw text. Reconstruct question-answer pairs by linking each analyst turn to the executive turns that follow it before the next analyst turn or operator handoff. Discard operator boilerplate, the safe-harbor paragraph (detected by a regex on "forward-looking statements" plus "Private Securities Litigation Reform Act" and removed as a block), and any turn under five tokens.

Speaker-tag beyond the vendor's role field: normalize titles into CEO, CFO, other executive, IR, analyst, operator. CEO and CFO tone should be modeled separately — the CFO's guidance language and the CEO's narrative language behave differently and we will test whether the CFO carries more of the signal.

**Step 2: domain-adapt a base model.** Start from a model that already knows financial English rather than training from scratch. The concrete recipe:

- Base: `deberta-v3-large` (He et al.), which outperforms BERT-family models on sentence classification with the same compute budget. Cross-check against a finance-pretrained encoder — FinBERT (Yang/Huang) or the Hugging Face `ProsusAI/finbert` — as a baseline to be beaten, not as the final model.
- **Domain-adaptive pretraining.** Continue masked-language-model pretraining of DeBERTa on our own transcript corpus — roughly 1.5–2 billion tokens across ten years — for 2–3 epochs, whole-word masking at 15%, learning rate 1e-5 with linear warmup. This is the "don't stop pretraining" result (Gururangan et al., 2020) and it reliably buys several points on downstream finance tasks. Hold out 2023–2024 transcripts from the MLM corpus as well, so that even the unsupervised stage never touches the final out-of-sample window. Cost: a few hundred GPU-hours on 4×A100, about $1–2k on a spot cloud instance.
- **Supervised fine-tuning.** Two heads, trained jointly with a shared encoder:
  1. *Tone head* — three-way (positive / neutral / negative) at the sentence level, initialized by fine-tuning on the Financial PhraseBank (Malo et al., 4,840 sentences, use the 100%-annotator-agreement subset for the clean signal and the 75% subset for volume) plus the SemEval-2017 Task 5 financial headlines set, then adapted with our own labels.
  2. *Uncertainty/hedging head* — binary, trained on sentences containing hedging constructions, so that "we feel reasonably comfortable with the low end of the range" is scored distinctly from confident language.
- **Our own labels.** Off-the-shelf corpora are headlines and news sentences; call language is different. Draw a stratified random sample of 6,000 sentences (stratified over year, sector, section, and speaker role) and have two annotators label each on a five-point tone scale plus a hedging flag, with a written codebook and a third adjudicator on disagreements. Target Krippendorff's alpha above 0.7; if the first 500 come in below that, fix the codebook before continuing. This is roughly two analyst-weeks or about $4–6k through a vetted annotation vendor with finance-literate annotators. Do not use undergraduate crowdworkers for this.
- **Weak supervision to multiply the labels.** Use the hand-labeled set as the gold evaluation set, and generate ~150k silver labels by prompting a strong general LLM with the same codebook and few-shot examples drawn from the gold set. Calibrate the LLM labeler against gold first and only keep it if it exceeds 0.75 macro-F1; train on silver, then do a final fine-tune pass on gold. This is the single highest-leverage use of the eight weeks — it is what separates a real domain model from a lexicon count.

**A deliberate refusal.** We are *not* fine-tuning the sentiment model on returns. It is tempting to train the encoder directly to predict forward returns, and it produces beautiful in-sample numbers, but it collapses the separation between the measurement instrument and the test — the model will learn to detect industry and time rather than tone, and every subsequent statistical test becomes meaningless. Tone is measured by a model trained only on tone labels. The link to returns is estimated afterward, in the open, with standard tools.

**Step 3: aggregate to call-level scores.** For each call produce, separately for prepared remarks and Q&A, and separately for CEO / CFO / all-management:

- `tone` = (Σ p_pos − Σ p_neg) / n_sentences, using predicted class probabilities rather than hard labels so that confidence is preserved.
- `uncertainty` = share of sentences flagged as hedging.
- `answer_evasion` = a Q&A-only measure: mean cosine similarity between an analyst's question embedding and the executive's answer embedding, plus the rate of non-answers (answer length under 25 words, or answer containing a deferral phrase). Low similarity = the question was dodged.
- `numeric_density` = count of numerals and units per hundred words in answers — concreteness as the inverse of evasion.

Also compute, as pure benchmarks to be beaten, the Loughran-McDonald dictionary tone and the Loughran-McDonald uncertainty count on the same text. If the fine-tuned model cannot beat LM on out-of-sample return prediction, that is a finding worth reporting honestly, and it is a real possibility.

**Step 4: peer-relativize and de-mechanize.** The raw score is contaminated by three things: industry-wide conditions, firm-specific speaking style, and the earnings news itself. Strip all three.

For each call *i* of firm *f* at date *t*:

1. **Firm baseline.** `Δtone = tone_i − mean(tone of the same firm's prior 4 calls)`. Do this per speaker where speaker continuity allows; when the CFO changes, reset the baseline and flag the observation, because a new CFO's tone shift is a speaking-style change, not news.
2. **Peer demeaning.** Subtract the equal-weighted mean Δtone of all peer-group firms that reported in the trailing 45 days, then divide by the cross-sectional standard deviation of that peer set, giving a z-score. Require at least five peer observations; otherwise fall back to the next-broader industry level and flag it.
3. **Orthogonalization to the known.** Regress the peer z-score cross-sectionally, each month, on standardized unexpected earnings, the three-day announcement return (−1,+1), guidance-change indicators, log size, book-to-market, 12-1 momentum, and idiosyncratic volatility. **The residual is the signal.** This is the whole ballgame: if the raw score predicts but the residual does not, we have found post-earnings drift wearing a costume, and we should say so plainly.

## Portfolio construction and evaluation

**Rebalancing on call dates, done honestly.** Signal is computed from the transcript, which is available within hours of the call. Entry price is the close of *t+1* where the call is post-close or intraday, and the close of *t+1* where the call is pre-open — i.e. always at least one full close after the transcript is public. Report a t+2 variant as the conservative case and a same-day-close variant as the aggressive bound; if the result only exists at same-day-close, it is not implementable and we say so.

Because calls arrive in clusters, run the portfolio as **overlapping cohorts** in the Jegadeesh-Titman style: at any date, hold an equal-weighted blend of the last K monthly cohorts, K ∈ {1, 2, 3}, so the strategy is fully invested and turnover is smoothed. Primary horizon: three months (K=3). Also report 1, 2, 6, and 12 months to trace decay — a real signal should decay monotonically and mostly die by twelve months; a signal that strengthens at twelve months is a red flag for a fluke.

**Sorts.** Quintiles on the residual signal, formed *within* peer group and then pooled, so the long-short book is industry- and size-neutral by construction rather than by later regression adjustment. Report both equal-weighted and value-weighted; value-weighted is the honest headline number because equal weighting loads on microcaps where the effect is easiest and least tradable. Also run a Fama-MacBeth cross-sectional regression at the call level as the continuous, non-discretized version of the same test — quintile sorts hide non-monotonicity, and Fama-MacBeth with Newey-West lags (3 lags for the 3-month horizon) gives a t-statistic on the slope that does not depend on the choice of five buckets.

**Risk adjustment.** Run the long-short monthly return series against: CAPM, Fama-French 3, Carhart 4, Fama-French 5, FF5+momentum, and q5. The headline is the FF5+MOM alpha with Newey-West standard errors. Also regress on a short-term-reversal factor, since a signal formed right after an announcement can accidentally be a reversal trade. Report the alpha, its t-statistic, the factor loadings, the information ratio, maximum drawdown, and the fraction of months positive.

**Cost model, made specific.** Per-share commission of $0.0015 plus a spread cost of half the time-weighted quoted spread from TAQ (or, if TAQ is out of budget, the Corwin-Schultz high-low spread estimator from CRSP, which is adequate at monthly frequency) plus a market-impact term using a square-root law: impact in basis points = 10 × σ_daily × sqrt(order size / ADV), capped by refusing any position exceeding 5% of 20-day median ADV. Short leg pays the actual borrow fee from Markit where available; where not, assume 40bp annualized for names above the size median and exclude the rest. Report gross and net alpha side by side, and report the **break-even cost** — the round-trip cost in basis points at which alpha reaches zero. If break-even is under 30bp, the strategy is a paper result.

Also compute capacity: the AUM at which net alpha halves under the impact model. A signal with a 20bp net alpha and $50m capacity is an intellectual finding, not a product, and the report should label it as such.

## Validation

This is where most of the eight weeks' credibility is earned, so it gets real time rather than a final afternoon.

**Temporal split, fixed on day one and never revisited.** Train and develop on 2015–2020. Validate (all model selection, threshold choice, horizon choice, peer-definition choice) on 2021–2022. **Touch 2023–2024 exactly once, at the end.** Write the final evaluation script before looking at the holdout and pre-register the primary specification — one signal, one horizon, one weighting scheme — in a dated file committed to the repo. Everything else is explicitly labeled secondary.

**Walk-forward.** Beyond the single split, run an expanding-window walk-forward: fit everything using data through year Y, trade year Y+1, roll. This produces a genuinely out-of-sample track record across the full decade and is the only version of the backtest I would show an investment committee.

**Model-quality validation, separate from return validation.** Report macro-F1 and per-class F1 on the held-out gold labels; a confusion matrix; and calibration (reliability diagram, expected calibration error). Check performance stability across sectors and years — if the model is 0.85 F1 on tech and 0.6 on utilities, the peer-relative scores are not comparable and we must standardize within sector more aggressively. Run an ablation table: LM dictionary → off-the-shelf FinBERT → domain-adapted DeBERTa → +Q&A separation → +speaker tags → +peer relativization → +orthogonalization. Each row should earn its complexity. If the table shows that the domain-tuned model adds nothing over LM once peer-relativization is in place, that is the most valuable single result of the study and it must be reported, not buried.

**Robustness battery.**
- Alternative peer definitions (GICS sub-industry, TNIC, FF48). The result should survive all three or we do not believe it.
- Subperiods: pre-COVID, 2020 (a legitimate stress test — tone collapsed everywhere, so peer-relativization is what should save the signal), post-2022.
- Size buckets: does anything survive in the largest 1000 names? If the alpha lives only in microcaps, say so.
- Drop the four weeks around March 2020 and confirm the result is not one crisis.
- Sector-neutral and country/exchange-neutral variants.
- Randomization test: shuffle the tone scores across firms within each date 1,000 times and confirm the true alpha sits outside the null distribution. This catches a surprising number of pipeline bugs.
- Placebo: use tone from the *next* call to predict the *prior* period's return. It should show nothing; if it shows something, there is a lookahead leak.

**Multiple-testing discipline.** We will inevitably examine dozens of specifications. Apply the Harvey-Liu-Zhu standard and treat a t-statistic below 3.0 on the primary specification as not established. Report a deflated Sharpe ratio adjusted for the number of specifications tried, and keep an honest count of that number in the repo.

## Pitfalls I would watch for

**The signal is post-earnings drift.** This is the single most likely outcome and the orthogonalization step is the defense. Watch specifically for the case where the residual signal's alpha collapses once the announcement return is controlled with a flexible (decile-dummy, not linear) specification — linear controls under-absorb drift and leave a residual that looks like alpha.

**Transcript timestamp errors.** A vendor field that gives publication rather than call time will silently let us trade on information hours before it existed. Audit 200 random calls by hand against the 8-K filing time on EDGAR. Any systematic bias here invalidates everything downstream.

**Survivorship and delisting.** Transcript coverage is itself survivorship-biased — dead companies' old transcripts sometimes get dropped from vendor archives. Reconcile the transcript universe against the CRSP universe each year and report coverage rates by size decile and year. If coverage of the smallest decile is 60% in 2015 and 90% in 2023, the time-series of the signal's breadth is an artifact.

**Look-ahead in the language model.** The base model's pretraining corpus extends past our test window; a model that has read 2024 news has, in a diffuse way, seen the future. This cannot be fully eliminated with a pretrained base, but it can be bounded: run the whole pipeline once with a base model whose pretraining cutoff precedes the test window and confirm the result direction holds. Also never let the MLM domain-adaptation stage see holdout-period transcripts.

**Boilerplate dominance.** Prepared remarks are heavily recycled; a firm that copies last quarter's script will show zero tone change by construction. Compute a quarter-over-quarter text similarity for prepared remarks and expect it to be high (0.6–0.8 cosine is normal). Use it as a control, and treat a sudden *drop* in similarity as its own candidate signal — an unusual amount of new script language is itself informative.

**Language drift over ten years.** Corporate vocabulary changed: "supply chain," "AI," "hybrid work" carry different valence in 2016 and 2023. A model trained on 2015–2020 will misread 2023 language. Mitigations: the walk-forward retraining, per-year standardization of scores, and an explicit diagnostic tracking mean model-predicted tone by year against a fixed anchor set of sentences.

**Managers know we are reading.** Firms increasingly manage call language with awareness that it is machined. Expect signal decay in later years — Cao, Jiang, Wang and Yang's work on "how to talk when a machine is listening" documents exactly this. Test for it directly: is the alpha in 2023–2024 significantly lower than 2015–2018? If yes, that is the honest forward-looking conclusion regardless of what the full-sample number says.

**Peer groups that are too small or too heterogeneous.** GICS sub-industries in the Russell 3000 can have three members. The minimum-five rule plus escalation to broader groupings handles this, but track the fraction of observations using the fallback; if it exceeds 20%, TNIC should become the primary classification.

**Crowding and cost sensitivity.** A three-month-horizon, call-date-triggered strategy is close to what many quant shops already run. Even a real alpha may be uninvestable after costs and borrow. Hence the break-even-cost number is a primary result, not an appendix.

**The result may be zero.** A clean, well-controlled null — "peer-relative call tone adds no incremental information beyond the earnings surprise and announcement return" — delivered with a credible ablation table and an honest out-of-sample test, is a genuinely useful output and should be written up with the same care as a positive one. The failure mode to avoid is spending week eight hunting for the specification that finally produces a t-statistic.

## Eight-week schedule

**Week 0 (start immediately, in parallel with everything):** data licences — S&P transcripts, I/B/E/S, CRSP/Compustat, Markit. Nothing else matters if these slip.

**Weeks 1–2.** Ingest and parse the full transcript corpus; build the speaker/section structure; join to CRSP/Compustat/IBES on a point-in-time basis; run the timestamp audit and the coverage reconciliation. Deliverable: a clean call-level panel with a data-quality memo. Freeze the temporal split and commit the pre-registration file.

**Week 3.** Annotation codebook, pilot 500 labels, measure agreement, revise, launch the 6,000-label build. In parallel, run MLM domain-adaptation on 2015–2022 transcripts.

**Week 4.** Fine-tune the tone and hedging heads; generate silver labels; evaluate on the gold set; produce the model-quality report and the ablation baseline against Loughran-McDonald. Gate: if macro-F1 on gold is below 0.70, spend the week fixing the model rather than moving on.

**Week 5.** Score the full corpus; build call-level scores, firm baselines, peer z-scores, and the orthogonalized residual. Deliverable: the signal panel plus its descriptive statistics — distribution, autocorrelation, correlation with SUE and announcement return.

**Week 6.** Portfolio construction, quintile sorts, Fama-MacBeth, factor regressions, cost model, on the 2015–2022 development and validation windows only.

**Week 7.** Robustness battery, walk-forward, randomization and placebo tests, capacity and break-even analysis. Fix whatever breaks.

**Week 8.** Single holdout run on 2023–2024 against the pre-registered specification. Write up. Report what the holdout said, whatever it said.

**Team and cost.** One quant researcher full-time, one data/ML engineer full-time for weeks 1–5 and half-time thereafter. Compute roughly $3–5k of GPU; annotation $4–6k; data licences dominate everything else and depend on existing subscriptions.

## What the deliverable looks like

A reproducible repo (raw → parsed → scored → signal → portfolio, each stage cached and re-runnable from a single command, with a `make all` that regenerates every number in the report), the annotated gold label set, the model card with the F1 and calibration numbers, the ablation table, the pre-registration file with its commit timestamp, and a memo whose first page states: the holdout alpha with its t-statistic and Newey-West standard error, net of the cost model; the break-even cost; the capacity estimate; and a plain statement of whether the signal is distinguishable from post-earnings drift. Everything else is supporting material.
