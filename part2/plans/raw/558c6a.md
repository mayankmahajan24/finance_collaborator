## The question, stated as a testable hypothesis

The claim we are testing is that the customers of a European financial institution leave a collective behavioral trace distinctive enough that the institution can be recognized from the trace alone. Concretely: if we take every anonymized institution ID in the panel, describe it purely by the aggregate behavior and demographics of the panel customers who hold accounts there, and group those descriptions, then (H1) the resulting groups will be sharply separated rather than a smear across a continuum; (H2) the groups will be stable — the same institutions land together when we resample customers or move to a different six-month window; and (H3) the axes that separate the groups will be interpretable as real institutional facts (country, charter type, business model, customer franchise), which lets us attach identities.

The null we are trying to beat is that institution IDs differ mainly in size and in the demographic mix of who happens to be in the panel, and that anything beyond that is noise we have dressed up as structure. That null is the thing to actually fear, and most of the validation below exists to kill it or to make us admit it.

Be honest with yourself about the target on day one. There is a ladder of specificity here: (1) country, (2) institution archetype — digital-native neobank, universal retail incumbent, savings/mortgage specialist, cooperative/mutual network, private/wealth bank, card monoline, e-money wallet or PSP, (3) named institution. Rungs 1 and 2 are genuinely reachable from behavior. Rung 3 is reachable only for the handful of institutions where an archetype cluster contains one member that is obviously the market leader in a country whose banking market is concentrated enough to make that unambiguous. Plan the whole exercise so it produces a defensible answer at rungs 1 and 2 with an explicit, calibrated escalation rule to rung 3, rather than producing forty confident names, most of which are wrong.

## Data required

Start from the transaction ledger joined to the panel roster. The minimum viable pull, per panel customer:

- Institution ID, account open/first-observed date, account type flag if present, currency, and the customer's country of residence.
- Demographics: age (or birth year), gender if present, self-reported or modeled income band, postcode district or NUTS-3 region, household size flag.
- Every transaction for 24 months: timestamp to the day (hour if available), signed amount in original currency and EUR-converted, direction, MCC where present, channel (card present / card not present / ATM / direct debit / standing order / instant transfer / SEPA credit transfer / cheque), card network as encoded (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, and the domestic schemes — Cartes Bancaires, girocard, Bancontact, Dankort, BankAxept, Multibanco, Bizum-linked instant transfers), merchant or counterparty ID where anonymized-but-consistent, and the counterparty institution ID for transfers where both legs are visible.
- Daily or month-end balance if available; otherwise a running balance reconstructed from a known anchor.

Two auxiliary datasets matter as much as the ledger. First, an external market-structure table: retail deposit and current-account market shares by country and institution, plus institution founding year, licence type and card-scheme issuing membership. ECB Statistical Data Warehouse, EBA transparency exercise, national central bank retail banking reports, and scheme membership lists are enough. Second, a national payments-habits table — cash share of POS, ATM withdrawals per capita, payday convention, instant-payment penetration — from the ECB SPACE study and national equivalents. These are what turn an unnamed cluster into a claim.

Filter before you model. Drop institution IDs with fewer than 200 panel customers or fewer than 30,000 transactions in the window; below that the aggregate features are dominated by sampling noise and will manufacture spurious clusters. Keep the dropped IDs in a holdout list — you will use them later as a scoring test, assigning them to the fitted clusters and checking whether their assignments are as stable as the training set's.

## Building the institutional signature

Compute features at the customer level first, then aggregate to the institution as a distribution, not just a mean. For each feature, carry the median, the interquartile range, and the share of customers above a fixed threshold. Means alone hide the thing that actually distinguishes institutions, which is the shape of the customer franchise.

**Salary and inflow timing.** For each customer, identify recurring credits: same counterparty, monthly cadence, amount stable within ±15% across at least four consecutive months, magnitude above the 60th percentile of that customer's credits. From those, derive the day-of-month distribution and compute (a) share of salary credits falling in the last two business days of the month, (b) share falling on the 25th–28th, (c) share on the 1st–3rd, (d) share arriving on a Friday, (e) the entropy of the day-of-month distribution, (f) share of customers with any detectable salary at all, and (g) median salary amount in EUR. The last-two-business-days pattern is heavily Southern-European and French; the 25th-of-month cluster is Nordic and Dutch; weekly and four-weekly Friday credits are British and Irish. Item (f) is the single best separator of primary current accounts from secondary/spending accounts, and it will do more work than anything else in the matrix.

**Payment mix.** Shares of debit-card, credit-card, direct-debit, standing-order, instant-transfer, and cash-withdrawal volume and count. Domestic-scheme share of card transactions, computed as a share of card transactions in the customer's home country to avoid contaminating it with travel. Contactless share, card-not-present share, and average card ticket size in EUR.

**Cash.** ATM withdrawals per customer-month, median withdrawal amount, share of total outflow taken as cash, and share of customers with zero ATM activity in a quarter. Germany and Austria sit high, the Nordics and the Netherlands near the floor, and within a country the cash-light tail is neobanks and second accounts.

**Balances.** Median month-end balance, the 90th percentile of month-end balance, the Gini coefficient of balance across the institution's customers, share of customer-months ending below EUR 50, and overdraft frequency. Balance dispersion separates mass-market retail from private banking far more cleanly than the mean does.

**Demographics.** Mean and IQR of age, share under 30, share over 65, share in the top income band, regional concentration measured as the Herfindahl index over NUTS-3 regions, and share of customers whose country of residence differs from the institution's modal country. The last one flags cross-border digital players.

**Franchise dynamics.** Share of accounts first observed in the last 12 months, median observed tenure, and monthly active rate. Fast-growing, young, low-balance, cash-free, high-CNP is a neobank fingerprint that is essentially unmistakable.

**Cross-institution topology.** This is the feature family most teams skip and it is the strongest one available. Panel customers who hold two or more institutions expose directed flows between institution IDs. Build the institution-by-institution transfer matrix and derive, per institution: net transfer position, share of its outflow going to other panel institutions, in-degree and out-degree, PageRank on the flow graph, and the fraction of its customers for whom it is their salary-receiving account versus a top-up destination. Salary hubs push money out to spending accounts; spending accounts receive. Also run a non-negative matrix factorization (k=6 to 10) on the co-holding matrix — which institutions are held together — because co-holding is dominated by country and by the incumbent/challenger pairing, and the loadings are excellent clustering inputs.

Standardize everything with a robust scaler (median and IQR, not mean and standard deviation), log-transform amounts and counts first, and prune features with pairwise Spearman |ρ| > 0.9, keeping the more interpretable member of each pair. Expect roughly 60–80 surviving features across perhaps 150–400 institutions — a wide, short matrix, so reduce before clustering: PCA to the number of components retaining 85% of variance, typically 8–14.

## Clustering

Do not bet on one algorithm. Run three, then reconcile.

1. **Ward hierarchical clustering** on Euclidean distance in PCA space, cutting at k = 2 through 15. Hierarchical is the right default because the true structure is plausibly nested — country inside archetype, or the reverse — and the dendrogram shows you which.
2. **Gaussian mixture models** with full covariance, k = 2 through 15, five restarts each, selecting k by BIC. GMM allows elongated, unequal-volume clusters, which matters because "universal incumbent" is a much broader region than "card monoline".
3. **HDBSCAN** with `min_cluster_size=5`, `min_samples=3`, on the same PCA embedding, which is the only one of the three willing to say "this institution belongs to nothing" — and some of them genuinely don't.

Reconcile by consensus: build a co-association matrix over the 30-odd partitions produced across algorithms and k values, counting the fraction of partitions in which each institution pair lands together, and run average-linkage clustering on 1 − co-association. Take the consensus partition as the primary result and report per-institution consensus strength (mean co-association with its own cluster) as a per-item confidence score. Use UMAP for the picture only. Never cluster on UMAP coordinates; it invents separation.

## Assigning identities

Once clusters exist, characterize before you name. Train a gradient-boosted classifier to predict cluster label from the pre-PCA features and take permutation importances, then, per cluster, compute standardized mean differences versus the rest of the sample and keep features with |SMD| > 0.8. That gives each cluster a two- or three-feature signature in the original, interpretable units.

Naming proceeds top-down. Country first, from the currency mix, the payday distribution, the domestic-scheme share, and the ATM intensity, benchmarked against the national payments-habits table — a country claim should be a match on at least three of those four, and disagreement means the cluster is cross-border, which is itself a finding. Archetype second, from the signature features against a pre-registered rubric written *before* you look at the clusters: e.g. neobank = under-30 share > 35% AND new-account share > 25% AND ATM outflow share < 5%; card monoline = credit-card share of outflow > 80% AND salary-detection rate < 10%; private bank = median balance above the 95th percentile of the sample AND over-65 share > 30%; retail incumbent = salary-detection rate > 55% AND direct-debit count per customer-month > 3. Writing the rubric first is what stops the naming step from becoming a story told about whatever came out.

Named institutions only at the third step, and only through a constrained assignment rather than free-hand guessing. Within a country-and-archetype cell, rank the member institution IDs by panel customer count and rank the known real institutions by retail current-account market share from the external table, then solve the assignment with the Hungarian algorithm on rank distance. Accept a named assignment only when the cell has at most three candidates, the panel share and market share ranks agree, and the gap between the top candidate and the runner-up in the objective exceeds a pre-set margin. Otherwise emit the archetype label and stop. Report every named claim with an explicit tier: Tier A (country + archetype, high confidence), Tier B (named, single dominant candidate), Tier C (named, ambiguous — listed as a candidate set, not an answer).

## Validation

Internal validation carries the whole burden here, so it has to be run as a gate, not a garnish.

**Separation.** Mean silhouette on the consensus partition, plus the per-cluster silhouette distribution and the Calinski-Harabasz index. Pre-commit the gate: mean silhouette below 0.25 means the structure is not real and the honest answer to the research question is no; 0.25–0.40 means weak but reportable structure at the archetype level only; above 0.40 supports the country-plus-archetype claim. Any individual cluster whose median silhouette is negative is dissolved and its members marked unassigned.

**Stability under resampling.** Hennig-style cluster-wise bootstrap: 500 iterations, each resampling *customers* with replacement (not institutions — resampling the unit of analysis tests the wrong thing; we want to know whether the signature is robust to which customers happened to be in the panel), recomputing all institution features from the resample, re-clustering, and computing the maximum Jaccard overlap between each original cluster and any bootstrap cluster. Clusters averaging above 0.75 are stable and reportable; 0.60–0.75 are provisional; below 0.60 are dissolved. Also record, per institution, the fraction of bootstraps in which it stays with its original cluster majority — that per-item number, not the cluster average, is what should govern whether we name that particular institution.

**Temporal agreement.** Split the 24 months into four non-overlapping six-month windows, rebuild features and re-cluster independently in each, and compute adjusted Rand index between window partitions on the institutions present in both. Pre-commit: ARI above 0.60 across adjacent windows supports the claim; below 0.40 means we are fitting period-specific noise. Watch specifically for the window containing December and August, where holiday spending and salary pre-payment distort timing features; if agreement collapses only in those windows, the fix is seasonal adjustment of the timing features, not abandonment.

**Null calibration.** Run the entire pipeline on 100 permuted datasets where customers are randomly reassigned to institutions while preserving institution size. Record the silhouette distribution under the null. If the observed silhouette is not above the 99th percentile of that null, everything above is decoration. This is the single most valuable check in the plan and takes an afternoon to write.

**Hand spot-checks.** For the five largest clusters, pull 20 randomly chosen customers each and read their twelve-month transaction narratives end to end — payday rhythm, employer counterparty stability, direct-debit portfolio, ATM behavior, where money goes when it leaves. Write a one-paragraph verdict per cluster before looking at the quantitative signature, then compare. Disagreement between the read and the signature is a finding worth more than any index.

## Pitfalls to watch

The dominant risk is that internal consistency measures cohesion, not correctness. A pipeline can produce beautifully separated, bootstrap-stable, time-consistent clusters of the wrong thing — the most likely wrong thing being account *role* (primary salary account, secondary spending account, savings pot) rather than institution identity, since role varies more within an institution than institution identity varies across them. Mitigate by computing role-purity within each cluster and, if a cluster is role-homogeneous but institution-heterogeneous, re-running the clustering on customers restricted to their primary account only. Report role-purity alongside silhouette every time.

Second: the panel is not the population. Panel recruitment skews young, urban, digital, and multi-banked, which inflates neobanks and under-represents cooperative and regional banks. Every market-share comparison in the naming step must be against panel-reweighted shares, and the report must state the direction of the bias.

Third: an institution ID may not be an institution. IDs can be BIN ranges, aggregator connection endpoints, sub-brands, or white-label programs on a sponsor bank's licence. A sponsor bank hosting five fintech programs looks like five institutions with identical infrastructure and different customers, or one institution with a bimodal franchise, depending on how the ID was minted. Check for suspiciously identical feature vectors across IDs and for IDs whose customer sets never overlap despite identical behavior.

Fourth: confirmatory naming. Once someone in the room says "cluster 3 is obviously the Spanish incumbent," every subsequent feature gets read as confirmation. The pre-registered rubric and the tiered output are the defenses; enforce them by having the rubric committed to the repo with a timestamp before the clustering is run.

Fifth: size confounds. Larger institutions have smoother, more central feature vectors purely from averaging, so they gravitate to cluster centers and small institutions to the periphery. Check the correlation between panel customer count and distance-to-centroid; if it exceeds 0.4, residualize the features on log customer count before clustering.

Sixth: leakage through currency and country. Country is so strong a signal that it will dominate the first two principal components and the clusters will simply be countries. That is a legitimate result for rung 1, but to reach rung 2 you must re-run the pipeline within each country, standardizing features against that country's own distribution, so archetype separation is not drowned by geography.

Finally, be ready to report a negative. If the permutation null is not beaten, or cluster-wise Jaccard sits below 0.6, or cross-window ARI is under 0.4, the deliverable is "behavioral signatures separate institutions by country and roughly by business model, but do not support identification of specific institutions," with the diagnostics attached. That is a real answer to the question asked, and a more useful one than a confident list that no one can check.
