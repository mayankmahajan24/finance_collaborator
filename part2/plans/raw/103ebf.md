## The shape of the problem

Someone has already told the market what we think we know. The channel checks were published by sell-side desks, distributed to their institutional clients, and in many cases summarized on Bloomberg and in the financial press within hours. So the interesting question is not "are the new logo wins real" — it is "what is left to earn." A correct read on the fundamentals that the market has already absorbed is worth zero. The plan therefore runs in two stages and is explicitly gated: stage one measures how much of the check-driven optimism is already in consensus numbers and in the stock price, and if the answer is "most of it," we stop and write a one-page note explaining why we passed. Only if a genuine gap survives stage one do we spend time on the second and harder question — whether management will actually put the strength into the guide, which is a question about their behavior, not about their business.

**Hypothesis (stated so it can fail):** New logo momentum reported by the channel is real but is (a) already reflected in consensus revenue and in the stock's relative performance since the notes went out, and (b) unlikely to convert into a full-year raise of a magnitude that surprises, because this management team's guidance policy is to hold or nudge the full year at this point in the fiscal calendar and reserve raises for later quarters. The long case requires *both* legs to be wrong. If either holds, there is no trade.

## Stage one — is it priced?

**Establish the information timeline first.** Before measuring anything, build a dated event log: for each sell-side note referencing the checks, record the publication date and time, the broker, the analyst's prior and post-note revenue/EPS estimates and price target, and whether the note was a rating change or a maintained rating with raised numbers. Sources: the broker research portals we have entitlements to (or the sales coverage directly — ask them to send the original PDFs with timestamps, not summaries), Bloomberg's research search (RES <GO>) and the ANR/EE screens, Refinitiv/LSEG or Visible Alpha for the estimate revision history, and Bloomberg's news tape (CN <GO> on the ticker) to see when the checks leaked into general circulation. The key output is a single "information date" — the first date the check thesis was in wide circulation — plus a secondary date if there was a later, louder wave. Do not assume the first note is the information date; often the second broker's note is the one that moved things because it added numbers.

**Measure consensus revision.** From the information date minus five trading days to today, compute the change in: (1) mean consensus revenue and EPS for the current quarter, (2) the same for the full fiscal year, (3) the high end and the dispersion (standard deviation across estimates, and the count of contributors). Also compute a "whisper proxy": the mean of estimates published in the last fifteen calendar days versus the stale full consensus, since the posted mean is contaminated by analysts who have not updated. Two thresholds matter. If full-year consensus revenue has moved up by more than roughly 0.5 percent and the recently-updated subset sits more than 1 percent above the stale mean, the buy side is already carrying above-consensus numbers and the "raise" is the expected case, not the surprise. If consensus is essentially unchanged (moves under 0.2 percent) and the recent-updater subset is not detached from the mean, the checks were noted and dismissed — which is either an opportunity or a signal that experienced analysts think the checks do not translate to revenue.

**Measure the price reaction.** Run a standard event study on the information date. Estimation window: 250 trading days ending 30 trading days before the information date. Model: a market-plus-industry two-factor regression of daily excess returns on the S&P 500 (or Russell 2000, matched to the name's size) and an equal-weighted basket of six to ten close comparables — same end-market, same business model, chosen before looking at the results and documented in the file so the basket cannot be tuned after the fact. Compute cumulative abnormal return over three windows: (-1, +1) around the information date to capture the immediate reaction, (0, +10) to capture the slower institutional absorption, and (0, today) to capture everything since. Compute the standard error of the CAR from the estimation-window residuals so we can say whether the move is statistically distinguishable from noise, and also express the CAR in dollars of market cap.

**Translate CAR into implied fundamentals — this is the step most people skip.** A price move is only interpretable against what it implies. Take the CAR-implied change in enterprise value and, holding the multiple constant, back out the implied change in forward revenue or ARR. Then compare that to the revenue the channel checks could plausibly support: number of new logos referenced, times a defensible average contract value from the company's own disclosure (total revenue divided by customer count, or the ACV they cite on calls), times the fraction of that ACV that lands in the current fiscal year given typical implementation and ramp lags. If the stock has already moved to price in more incremental revenue than the checks can arithmetically deliver, the trade is not a long — it may be the opposite. This calculation, done honestly with an explicit ramp assumption, is usually the whole answer.

**The gate.** Proceed to stage two only if: full-year consensus revision is under roughly 0.5 percent, the (0, today) CAR versus the comp basket is under roughly one estimation-window standard deviation and under the CAR that the arithmetic above says the wins are worth, and the options market is not already pricing an outsized print (compare the straddle-implied earnings-day move to the trailing eight-quarter realized absolute earnings-day moves; if implied is more than about 1.2 times the realized median, the event is crowded). If two of these three fail, stop. Write the pass note and move on. Passing is the most common correct outcome here and the plan should treat it as a success, not a failure.

## Stage two — will guidance actually move?

If we get through the gate, the question becomes whether the print delivers a raise large enough to matter. Treat the guide as a decision management makes, subject to their own institutional habits and incentives, rather than as a readout of the quarter.

**Build the guidance history table.** Go back at least twelve quarters, ideally sixteen or through the current CFO's full tenure, using 10-Qs, 8-K press releases, and earnings call transcripts. For every quarter record: the guided range for the quarter and the full year as given at the time; the actual result; the beat in percent and in dollars versus both guidance midpoint and consensus; whether the full-year guide was raised, maintained, or cut at that print; and critically, the *raise-to-beat ratio* — if they beat the quarter by twenty million, did they raise the full year by twenty million (pass-through), by less (partial, i.e., sandbagging the back half), or by more (a genuine acceleration call). Also record the language: pull the exact sentences on the guidance philosophy from each call, and tag them as "prudent/conservative," "reflects current visibility," or "assumes no improvement in macro."

**Extract the policy.** From that table, answer specific questions. Does this company ever raise the full year at the fiscal quarter in question? Compute the base rate: of the last twelve first-quarter (or whichever quarter this is) prints, how many included a full-year raise, and what was the median size in percent of full-year revenue? Many management teams have a near-absolute policy of not touching the full year at Q1 regardless of the quarter's strength — if this is one of them, the trade thesis dies here no matter how good the checks are. What is the median raise-to-beat pass-through? A team that consistently passes through 50 percent or less of a beat will not deliver a "meaningful boost" in the sense the question means. Has the pattern broken recently — has the pass-through ratio drifted, and did it change when the CFO changed? A CFO in their first year almost always guides more conservatively than a tenured one, and this single fact overturns more guidance-cadence backtests than anything else.

**Check whether the mechanism even reaches the guide.** New logos are a bookings event; guidance is a revenue statement. Establish the lag: for a subscription or recurring-revenue business, work out from disclosures the typical time from signature to revenue recognition (implementation period, ramp schedule, whether contracts start at full ACV or step up), and the revenue backlog/RPO disclosure in the 10-Q. If new logos signed this quarter contribute revenue starting two or three quarters out, then even a genuine logo surge shows up in RPO and current deferred revenue this print but barely touches the current fiscal year's revenue line — which means the correct expectation is strong RPO, unchanged or modestly raised revenue guide, and a stock reaction that depends entirely on whether the market reads RPO or reads the guide. Also size the new logos against the base: if the company adds four hundred customers a quarter, "incrementally positive on new logos" may be worth a fraction of a percent of revenue and is inside the noise of net revenue retention, churn, and expansion — which are much larger swing factors and which channel checks on new logos say nothing about.

**Model it.** Build three scenarios for the print with explicit probabilities anchored on the base rates above: no full-year change (probability from the historical base rate for this quarter), a token raise roughly equal to the quarter's beat (pass-through case), and a raise above the beat. For each, estimate the stock reaction using the company's own history — regress past earnings-day abnormal returns on the size of the full-year guidance revision across the twelve-quarter sample to get an empirical sensitivity (percent stock move per percent of guidance raise), rather than guessing. Expected value of the long is the probability-weighted reaction net of the pre-print drift we already measured in stage one. Require a materially positive expected value — not merely positive — before recommending it, and size against the straddle so we know we are not paying more for the event than it can pay us.

## Validation

Every number in stage one should be reproduced two ways. Pull consensus from two independent vendors (Bloomberg and Visible Alpha, or FactSet) and reconcile the revision figures; they differ on which brokers are in the composite and on how stale estimates are handled, and a revision that appears in one and not the other is usually a contributor-set artifact. Re-run the event study with a different comp basket and with a plain market model to confirm the CAR sign and rough magnitude are not an artifact of factor choice; if the conclusion flips between specifications, the honest answer is that the price move is not measurable and we should weight the estimate-revision evidence instead.

Validate the guidance table by having a second person independently re-extract three randomly chosen quarters from the primary filings; guidance transcription errors — especially confusing the guide as originally given with the guide as restated after a segment reclassification or an accounting change — are the most common defect in this kind of work. Restated periods must be handled by using the guidance as given at the time against the results as reported at the time, never against restated actuals.

Back-test the guidance policy finding out of sample: if the pattern is "never raises at this quarter," check whether that predicted correctly in the two most recent instances and whether the stock reaction on those days matched what the sensitivity regression implies. Also run the falsification directly — if the checks are informative, similar check-driven episodes in this name's past should have preceded guidance raises. Find the two or three prior occasions when sell-side published positive checks on this company, and check what actually happened at the following print. A hit rate near zero is the most useful thing this plan can produce.

Finally, seek disconfirming information rather than confirming: talk to the analysts who did *not* raise numbers and ask why, look for a competitor's recent commentary that contradicts the demand picture, and check whether the logo wins came at a discount (pricing pressure shows up as flat revenue on rising customer counts, visible in revenue per customer over eight quarters).

## Pitfalls to watch

The channel checks are a sample of unknown construction. A sell-side check is typically eight to twenty conversations with partners, resellers, or ex-employees, selected non-randomly, often by an analyst who already has a view and a rating to defend. There is no denominator. Treat the check as a directional signal with a wide error bar, never as a measurement, and never rebuild a revenue model on it.

Do not confuse a stock that has gone up with a stock that has priced something in — the CAR must be measured against comps and factors, because half of a 6 percent move in a high-beta software name over three weeks is usually just the tape. Conversely, do not conclude "unpriced" from a flat stock if the sector sold off hard over the same window; the relevant comparison is always relative.

Beware the sandbag trap in reverse: a company known for conservatism can be one whose conservatism is fully understood and therefore already in buy-side numbers, so the "beat and small raise" that looks like a positive is exactly what everyone expected and the stock falls. The buy-side bar, not consensus, is what the stock trades against; the straddle and the recently-updated estimate subset are our best proxies for that bar and both should be checked.

Watch for confounds in the event window — a competitor's earnings, a large sector ETF flow, an index rebalance, a lock-up expiry, an analyst day, or a rate move that repriced long-duration growth all contaminate the CAR. Log every dated event in the window and, if one lands, shorten the window or exclude the day and report both.

Watch for look-ahead and selection bias in the guidance table: choose the comp basket and the number of historical quarters before running anything, and do not drop quarters as "not comparable" after seeing that they break the pattern (a quarter with an acquisition genuinely needs a note, but the decision rule for exclusion must be written first).

Be honest about what the second stage can and cannot deliver. Twelve to sixteen observations of a binary guidance decision is a small sample; a "raises at this quarter three times out of twelve" base rate has a confidence interval wide enough to drive a truck through. Report it as such rather than as a 25 percent probability stated to two decimals. And remember the asymmetry that makes this whole setup dangerous: if the checks are right and priced, the long makes little; if the checks are right and management does not raise, the long loses; if the checks are wrong, the long loses badly. Three of four states are bad. That asymmetry, not any single number in this plan, is the reason the default answer is no.

Finally, before touching any of this, clear the sourcing question with compliance. Channel checks that involve former employees or channel partners can shade into material non-public information, and expert-network calls arranged around a print carry their own restrictions. Confirm the notes are broadly distributed research and that nothing in our own diligence puts us in possession of something the market does not have.
