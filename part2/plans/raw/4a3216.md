## The question, stated as something falsifiable

We hold a panel of European retail customers. For each customer we see demographics (age, sex, income band, home postcode) and a transaction stream in which counterparty and account-provider institutions appear only as opaque IDs (`INST_8827`, `INST_0413`). The question is whether those IDs can be mapped to named, real institutions — Deutsche Bank, Rabobank, CaixaBank, Crédit Agricole Nord de France — with enough reliability that the mapping can be used in downstream analysis without poisoning it.

**Hypothesis.** For each country, the observable behaviour of an institution's panel customers carries a signature — how many customers it has, where they live, how old and how rich they are, when their salaries land, when state benefits arrive, what the monthly account fee is, how fast the customer base grew — that is close to unique among the institutions actually operating in that country. Formally: the map from real institutions to behavioural fingerprints is injective for a substantial share of the retail market, and the injective share is larger in concentrated markets (Netherlands, Sweden) than in fragmented ones (Germany, Austria, Italy).

**Null hypothesis we are genuinely trying to reject.** The fingerprints are not separable: the 350-odd German Sparkassen, or the dozens of Spanish former-cajas, occupy overlapping regions of feature space, and any apparent identification is threshold-fitting noise that will not survive being tested on institutions the analyst has never seen.

**Success criterion, fixed before any modelling.** Per country, report the share of panel deposit volume that can be assigned to a named institution at ≥95% precision, measured on institutions held out entirely from model development. If that share is below 40% in a country, we report the country as not identifiable and say so plainly. A high-precision map covering 60% of volume plus an honest "unknown" bucket for the rest is a usable deliverable; an 80%-coverage map at 70% precision is not.

## Structural commitment: two registers that never touch

Everything downstream depends on one discipline, so it goes first.

Every variable in the project is assigned to exactly one of two registers, recorded in a machine-readable manifest (`registers.yaml`) checked at import time.

**Register A — model inputs.** Anything computed from panel transactions and panel demographics. Panel customer counts per ID, geographic distribution of those customers, age/income distributions, day-of-month credit spikes, recurring fee amounts, card scheme mix, growth curves, ATM-withdrawal geography inferred from merchant-location fields.

**Register B — anchors.** Anything published by a third party that had no access to the panel. Never used to build, weight, calibrate, or select features. Used only to score a proposed identification and to adjudicate it.

The two registers meet exactly once, in the scoring step, and never in the other direction. A unit test walks the feature-construction call graph and fails the build if any Register B symbol is reachable. This sounds bureaucratic; it is the single thing that separates a real result from a circular one, and in my experience it is violated accidentally within about two weeks unless it is enforced by code rather than by intention.

## Data required

**Panel side (Register A).** Per country, transaction-level records for ≥18 consecutive months, with: pseudonymous customer ID; account-provider institution ID; counterparty institution ID where the counterparty is a financial institution; timestamp to the day; signed amount in EUR/local; MCC or merchant category; merchant name string if available; transaction type (SEPA credit transfer, direct debit, card POS, ATM, internal). Per customer: age, sex, income band, residential postcode aggregated to NUTS-3 (finer is a privacy liability and buys little). Minimum viable panel: 30,000 customers per country; realistically we want 100,000+ in Germany because the tail is long.

**Anchor side (Register B).** Concretely, and in the order I would acquire them:

1. **Institution roster.** ECB RIAD / the MFI register (list of monetary financial institutions), filtered to entities holding household deposits, joined to LEIs. This gives the candidate set and, importantly, its legal-entity structure. Time-versioned: pull the register vintage matching each quarter of the panel.
2. **Deposit market share.** ECB Consolidated Banking Data (CBD2) for group-level totals; national sources for household-deposit shares, which are what we actually want: Bundesbank *Bankenstatistik*; De Nederlandsche Bank statistics tables; Banco de España *Boletín Estadístico*; Banca d'Italia base dati statistica; ACPR/Banque de France for France; KNF for Poland. Supplement with the EBA Transparency Exercise, which gives per-bank retail exposures by country of counterparty — unusually good for cross-border cases.
3. **Branch and ATM footprints.** ECB Payments Statistics (PSD) for national totals; OpenStreetMap `amenity=bank` / `amenity=atm` with the `operator` and `brand` tags for spatial distribution — extract via Overpass, geocode to NUTS-3, and treat the operator tag's coverage rate as a known-missingness problem rather than assuming completeness. National supplements where they exist (Germany's Sparkassen and Volksbanken publish branch directories; Spanish banks publish office lists in annual reports).
4. **Published tariff and rate schedules.** German *Preis- und Leistungsverzeichnis*, Dutch tarievenoverzicht, Spanish *folleto de tarifas*. These give exact monthly account fees (€0.00, €3.90, €4.90, €10.90…), card fees, and out-of-network ATM charges. Fee amounts are close to a serial number and are, crucially, published independently of anything we compute.
5. **Payroll and state-benefit calendars.** Payment dates for Kindergeld (Familienkasse, staggered by the last digit of the Kindergeldnummer), Dutch AOW and toeslagen (fixed monthly dates), French CAF and pension payments, Spanish Seguridad Social pension dates, Italian INPS. These discriminate on customer composition — pensioner-heavy regional banks versus salary-account neobanks — more than on the institution directly, which is exactly the axis where size and geography are weakest.
6. **Public customer-count and growth disclosures.** Annual reports and press releases for N26, Revolut, bunq, Nickel, ING, and the incumbents. Growth curves are among the most discriminating anchors for the 2019–2024 window, because neobank trajectories are steep and distinct.

## Method

**Step 1 — Fingerprint construction, per country, per institution ID, per quarter.** All from Register A:

- `n_share`: panel customers with an active account at the ID, divided by panel customers with any account, post-stratification weighted (raking on NUTS-2 × age band × sex × income decile to Eurostat population margins).
- `geo`: the vector of customer shares over NUTS-3 regions, Laplace-smoothed.
- `age_dist`, `inc_dist`: quantile summaries plus full histograms.
- `payday`: normalised histogram of large inbound credit dates over the month, plus a separate histogram restricted to credits classified as salary (recurring, same counterparty, ≥60% of monthly inflow).
- `benefit_mix`: share of customers receiving a credit on each country-specific benefit payment date.
- `fee_signature`: the modal set of small recurring debits from the institution to the customer — the account fee, in cents.
- `scheme_mix`: Visa/Mastercard/Maestro/Girocard/Bancontact/Cartes Bancaires shares.
- `growth`: log customer count by quarter, normalised to the panel's own growth.
- `atm_geo`: NUTS-3 distribution of the customer's ATM withdrawals, which tracks branch footprint more tightly than residence does for regional banks.

**Step 2 — Resolve the entity level.** Before matching, decide what an ID *is*. Anonymized IDs are often BIC-level or product-level, while anchors are group-level. Cluster IDs within a country using a conservative rule — IDs whose customers overlap heavily at the customer level (same person holding both), share a fee signature, and share a geography — and treat the cluster as the matching unit. Symmetrically, build the roster hierarchically: group → legal entity → brand, so a match can be declared at whatever level the evidence supports. "This ID is a Sparkasse" is a real, useful, defensible answer; "this ID is Sparkasse Hannover" may not be.

**Step 3 — Score matrix.** For country *c*, build `S[i,r]` = log-likelihood ratio that ID *i* is institution *r*, summed over independent comparators:

- Size: `log10(n_share_i / deposit_share_r)` scored under a Gaussian with mean μ_c and σ_c, where μ_c absorbs systematic panel skew (an app-based panel over-represents neobanks by roughly a factor of 3–8) and σ_c is estimated from the labelled training institutions. Estimating rather than assuming μ_c is the difference between a size comparator that works and one that rejects every incumbent.
- Geography: Jensen–Shannon divergence between `geo_i`/`atm_geo_i` and the branch-footprint distribution of *r*, mapped to a log-odds via a logistic fit on training pairs.
- Fee: exact-match bonus if the observed modal fee equals a published tariff line, with a heavy penalty for contradiction. High information, near-zero cost.
- Demographic: Wasserstein distance on age and income against published customer profiles where they exist (rarely), otherwise against the institution's *type* prior (Sparkassen skew older and rural; neobanks skew 25–35 urban).
- Growth: correlation between the ID's quarterly log-growth and the institution's disclosed customer growth.
- Timing: divergence between `benefit_mix` and what the institution's regional/demographic footprint implies.

**Step 4 — Joint assignment with an explicit abstain option.** Do not take per-ID argmaxes; the constraint that two IDs cannot both be Rabobank carries a lot of information. Solve a min-cost flow on the bipartite graph with (a) an abstain sink attached to every ID at fixed cost τ_c, (b) capacities >1 on institutions known to plausibly emit multiple IDs, and (c) a "not in roster" sink for foreign banks and BaaS-sponsored fintechs. Solve the hard assignment with Jonker–Volgenant (`scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment` on the augmented matrix) for the point estimate, and obtain posterior marginals `p(i→r)` by entropic optimal transport (Sinkhorn, ε tuned so the entropy matches a Gibbs sampler run on a small country) or by direct Metropolis sampling over assignments in countries small enough to afford it.

**Step 5 — Abstain rule.** Accept the identification only if `max_r p(i→r) ≥ 0.80` **and** the margin over the runner-up is ≥ 0.25 **and** at least three comparators independently favour the winner **and** the ID has ≥300 panel customers in the quarter (below that, geography is too noisy to mean anything). Everything else returns `UNKNOWN` with a reason code: `insufficient_evidence`, `degenerate_class`, `no_roster_candidate`, `anchor_conflict`. Thresholds are then *recalibrated*, not assumed: fit Venn–Abers or split-conformal calibration on training institutions to convert the raw posterior into a probability with coverage guarantees, and choose the operating point as the one that achieves 95% precision on training folds. The numbers above are the starting point and the fallback if calibration data is too thin.

## Validation

**Ground truth.** We need institutions whose true identity is known through a channel independent of the model. Three sources, in descending order of quality: (1) a consented survey of a panel subsample asking "who do you bank with", which yields ID→name labels directly, targeting 500 respondents per country; (2) institutions the data provider is contractually able to reveal as seeds; (3) unambiguous public cases — a national payment scheme or a single-brand market with one legal entity. Target 25–60 labelled institutions per country. If we cannot reach 15, the country cannot be validated and should be reported as such rather than modelled anyway.

**The holdout unit is the institution, never the transaction, never the customer.** Splitting by transaction leaks catastrophically: the same institution appears in train and test, and the model memorises its fingerprint instead of learning the mapping rule. Concretely: leave-one-institution-out over labelled institutions. For each fold, hide the held-out institution's label, refit all comparator weights, μ_c, σ_c, the logistic geography map, and the calibration/thresholds on the remaining institutions only, then re-run the full joint assignment with the held-out institution's roster row still present and score the prediction. Because thresholds are selected inside the fold, the outer estimate stays honest — this is nested CV, and skipping the nesting is the most common way this class of project overstates itself by 10–20 points of precision.

**A second, harder split: leave-one-country-out.** Fit everything on four countries, apply to the fifth with only that country's anchors. This measures whether the *method* transfers or whether we merely fitted five separate countries. Report both numbers; the gap between them is the honest measure of how much per-country hand-tuning is doing.

**Metrics, per institution and per country.** Precision = correct / (predicted, non-abstained). Recall = correct / (all true labelled institutions). Coverage = non-abstained share, reported twice: unweighted, and weighted by panel deposit volume, because identifying the top five banks is worth more than identifying the tail. Plot the full risk–coverage curve rather than quoting a single point. Also report top-3 accuracy (does the truth appear in the ranked shortlist?), which tells us whether failures are near-misses or nonsense, and a confusion structure over institution *types* — if the model confuses Sparkassen with each other but never with neobanks, the class-level answer is still shippable.

**Pilot countries.** Netherlands (concentrated, ~3 banks at 80%+ — the sanity check; if it fails here, stop), Germany (fragmented, mutual/savings sectors — the hard case), Spain (regional structure plus recent merger churn), France (mutual groups with regional legal entities), Poland (mid-concentration, non-euro, tests currency and calendar generalisation).

**Negative controls, run before believing anything.** (a) Permute the ID→fingerprint mapping within country and confirm precision collapses to chance. (b) Inject three synthetic "phantom" IDs with fabricated fingerprints and confirm all three abstain — a system that never abstains is not calibrated, it is guessing. (c) Drop the largest single comparator and confirm the result degrades gracefully rather than vanishing, which would reveal a single-feature model wearing an ensemble's clothes.

## Pitfalls I would watch for, and the countermeasure for each

**Circularity through the back door.** Some "published" market shares are themselves built from transaction panels — several commercial market-share estimates are derived from card or aggregator data that may share a lineage with our panel. Restrict anchors to regulator- and institution-published primaries; document provenance for every anchor row; exclude any vendor estimate whose methodology is not disclosed.

**Panel skew treated as measurement error.** The panel is not a random sample of Europeans. It skews young, urban, smartphone-native, and neobank-heavy. If we compare raw panel share to national deposit share and call the difference noise, we will systematically match incumbents to challengers. Model the skew explicitly with a per-institution-type selection term estimated on labelled institutions, and post-stratify. Then check the residual skew on held-out institutions; if it is not stable, the size comparator should be downweighted or dropped, not patched.

**Anchor vintage versus panel vintage.** European retail banking has been consolidating throughout any plausible panel window: CaixaBank absorbed Bankia, Unicaja absorbed Liberbank, French and German mutual sectors keep merging local entities. Matching 2023 panel behaviour to 2020 deposit statistics produces confident wrong answers. Version every anchor by quarter and enforce a temporal join; treat merger events as roster transitions where one ID may legitimately map to two names on either side of a date.

**Genuine degeneracy, and the temptation to break ties anyway.** Some institutions are not distinguishable by anything we can observe — two similarly sized regional savings banks in adjacent, demographically identical NUTS-3 areas. The correct output is an abstain with `degenerate_class` and a named candidate set, not a coin flip dressed as a prediction. Detect degeneracy structurally by inspecting the Sinkhorn marginals for near-ties, and report the size of each equivalence class as a headline result — "27% of German panel volume resolves only to sector, not entity" is a finding, not a failure.

**Truth outside the roster.** A consumer brand may sit on a sponsor bank's licence (Solaris, Railsbank-style arrangements), or be a foreign passported entity absent from the national register. Without a `not_in_roster` sink, the assignment will force these onto an innocent domestic bank. Keep the sink, and prior it from the known share of e-money and passported activity in each country.

**Tourism and commuting distortions in geography.** ATM and card geography in the Balearics, the Côte d'Azur, Tyrol, and border regions reflects visitors, not customers. Downweight NUTS-3 regions with high tourism intensity (Eurostat nights-spent data) and cross-border commuting flows when computing the geography divergence.

**Weak, correlated timing features.** Payday clustering on the last banking day is nearly universal and therefore nearly uninformative; benefit-date features are informative about customers, not institutions, and will double-count with the demographic comparator. Estimate the comparator covariance on training institutions and score with a multivariate likelihood rather than summing log-odds as if independent — otherwise confidence is inflated exactly where it is least warranted.

**Threshold overfitting and the winner's curse.** With 25–60 labelled institutions per country, it is trivially easy to tune τ_c into a precision number that does not replicate. Nested CV, no peeking at the outer fold, and report the outer estimate with a bootstrap interval over institutions — never over transactions, which would understate the interval by an order of magnitude.

**Governance, which is gate zero and not an afterthought.** Deliberately de-anonymizing a provider's data may breach the data licence, and re-identification analyses touch GDPR even when the entities identified are companies rather than people. Before any code runs: confirm with legal and with the data provider that institution-level identification is permitted; document that no output identifies an individual; set a minimum cell size (no institution reported with fewer than 300 panel customers, no geography cell below 50); and agree in advance who may see the resulting crosswalk. If the licence forbids this, the project stops on Monday morning, and that is a legitimate outcome to have discovered cheaply.

## Six-week schedule

*Week 1:* legal and licence clearance; register manifest and leakage test in CI; roster assembly from the MFI register for five countries; survey fielded for ground-truth labels. *Week 2:* anchor acquisition — deposit statistics, OSM footprints, tariff schedules, benefit calendars — with provenance and vintage recorded. *Week 3:* fingerprint pipeline and entity-level clustering; negative controls (a) and (b) built before any real run. *Week 4:* score matrix, min-cost flow with sinks, Sinkhorn marginals; Netherlands sanity check end to end. *Week 5:* nested leave-one-institution-out across all five countries; calibration; risk–coverage curves. *Week 6:* leave-one-country-out transfer test; degeneracy-class report; written findings, including the countries where the answer is that this does not work.

## What we will be able to say at the end

Either: "In the Netherlands, Spain, and France we identify institutions covering X% of panel deposit volume at 95% precision on held-out institutions, with the remainder explicitly marked unknown; in Germany, entity-level identification fails but sector-level identification succeeds at Y% coverage" — or the honest negative, which is that behaviour plus demographics does not separate European institutions once you stop grading yourself on institutions you have already seen. Both are publishable internally. Only the first is usable downstream, and only if the abstain class travels with it.
