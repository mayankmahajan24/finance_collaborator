# c761e5

## The question, restated so it can be answered with numbers

The claim on the tape is qualitative: partners and resellers say new logo activity is better. The decision in front of us is quantitative and has two separable parts. First, how many dollars of fiscal-year revenue can a plausible new-logo upside actually produce, and is that number large enough to move a guidance range that management sets by policy rather than by arithmetic? Second, if the in-year number is small but the exiting-ARR number is large, is that a reason to own the stock for twelve months even if it is not a reason to own it into the print?

Keeping those two apart is most of the work. Analysts lose money here by proving the second and trading the first.

## Hypothesis

Primary hypothesis, stated so it can fail: the incremental new-logo activity described in the channel work, taken at full face value and with no haircut, adds less than 75 basis points to fiscal-year revenue versus the current guidance midpoint, which is inside the noise band of a typical guidance range and below the threshold at which this management team has historically raised beyond simple flow-through of a quarterly beat. If that is right, the print is not the catalyst the notes imply, and any long has to be underwritten on next-year ARR, not on this year's guide.

Alternate hypothesis to be taken equally seriously: the company's revenue model is consumption-weighted or its land sizes are large and fast-ramping, in which case in-year sensitivity to new logos is several times higher, the checks are directly relevant to the guide, and the trade is live. The model below is built to distinguish these two cases rather than to assume one.

The single number the whole exercise produces: implied fiscal-year revenue, with a distribution around it, expressed in basis points versus both the guidance midpoint and the current consensus line — plus the same for exiting ARR and next-year revenue.

## Data to pull Monday morning

From the company: the last twelve 10-Qs and three 10-Ks, every quarterly ARR or annualized-recurring-revenue disclosure, total customer count and the count of customers above whatever spend threshold is disclosed (typically $100k), RPO and current RPO, deferred revenue and the change in unbilled receivables, net and gross retention, services revenue as a separate line, and the geographic and segment splits. If the company IPO'd within the last five years, the S-1 and any investor-day deck almost certainly contain a cohort chart showing dollar-based expansion by signing year — that chart is the single most valuable input in this entire plan and it is usually never updated afterward, which is exactly why it is under-used. Pull every earnings call transcript for three years and tag each one for guidance language.

From consensus: line-item consensus (Visible Alpha or equivalent) for revenue by segment and by quarter, ARR, cRPO, customer adds, and operating margin; the sell-side model files themselves for the three most-read analysts on the name; and the thirty-day and ninety-day estimate revision trend so you can see whether the channel notes have already been put into published numbers or are sitting only in whisper. Ask the desk explicitly what the buy-side bogey is versus the printed consensus; the gap between those two is where the money is made or lost and it is not in any database.

From the channel, independently: do not rely on the sell-side write-ups. Book four to six expert calls — two former sales leaders who carried a quota at the company within the last eighteen months, two current channel partners or systems integrators (ideally one who is losing share to a competitor, so the sample is not entirely self-selected winners), and one procurement-side buyer who ran a recent competitive evaluation. Ask each of them for counts, not adjectives: deals registered this quarter versus the same quarter last year, close rates, average first-year contract value, discounting versus a year ago, and implementation start dates for what they closed last quarter.

Alternative and observable data: quota-carrying-rep headcount and implementation/professional-services headcount from LinkedIn history and job-posting archives; cloud-marketplace listings and private-offer activity on AWS, Azure and GCP; G2 and review-site velocity; installed-base tracking from HG Insights or Enlyft; and, if the customer base skews small, a card panel. Implementation and professional-services headcount deserves special attention: it is the cleanest public tell that lands are actually converting into recognizable revenue rather than sitting as signed paper.

Market data: implied move from the front expiry straddle, realized moves for the last eight prints, short interest and borrow, and the stock's performance since the channel notes were published.

## Building the model

Start by reconstructing reported revenue from the ground up for the last twelve quarters, and do not proceed until the reconstruction ties to within roughly one percent each quarter. The identity is: quarterly subscription revenue equals revenue from the installed base at the start of the quarter, adjusted for gross churn and expansion timing, plus the in-quarter contribution of every cohort landed in the trailing eight quarters, plus services. Everything downstream depends on getting this rebuild to tie, because a model that cannot reproduce the past has no standing to forecast the future.

Estimate initial land value rather than assuming it. Net new ARR in a quarter equals new-logo ARR plus expansion ARR minus churned and downgraded ARR. Expansion and churn can be backed out of the disclosed net and gross retention rates applied to the trailing-twelve-month-lagged base. What remains is new-logo ARR; divide by the quarter's logo adds to get average initial land. Do this for twelve quarters and look at the trend — the level matters less than whether land sizes have been drifting down, which is the most common way a "more logos" story turns out to be revenue-neutral.

Then calibrate the ramp curve, which is the heart of the model. Define R(k) as the revenue recognized in the k-th quarter after signing, expressed as a multiple of one quarter of the initial land value (so a logo that goes live instantly and never expands has R(0) = 1.0). Two parameters matter most: the signature-to-go-live lag, and the cohort expansion path. For the lag, a cohort signed uniformly across a ninety-day quarter with an L-day implementation delay is live on average for (45 − L) days of that quarter, so R(0) is approximately max(0, (45 − L)/90). At a forty-five-day lag, R(0) is zero. At a sixty-day lag, the first full quarter of revenue is not until the quarter after next. For the expansion path, seed R(4) through R(12) from the S-1 or investor-day cohort chart, converted from annual to quarterly. Then let a solver fit L and the expansion path jointly by minimizing squared error against the twelve quarters of reported revenue, with L constrained to a range you can defend from the expert calls and services-revenue seasonality. Write down the fitted L and show it to the experts; if they say implementations take four months and your solver says three weeks, the model is wrong, not them.

Now shock it. Translate the channel commentary into a logo count with an explicit chain of arithmetic, each link visible. If eight partners were checked and they represent, say, fifteen percent of new-logo sourcing, and they report deal registrations up twenty-five percent year over year against a ten percent comparison in the prior quarter, then apply the delta only to the partner-sourced share of logos, not to the whole. Convert registrations to closed-won using the historical close rate from the same partners, not a blended one. Then haircut for selection bias — partners who take a call are disproportionately the ones having a good quarter — by applying a credibility weight of roughly one half in the base case, with the full-credit version run as the bull case. Carry the resulting logo delta at the assumed average land value, but also run a version where the incremental logos land twenty-five percent below the trailing average, which is what usually happens when volume accelerates.

Push the shocked cohort through the ramp curve and read off three outputs: the incremental fiscal-year revenue in dollars and in basis points versus the guide midpoint and versus consensus; the incremental exiting ARR; and the incremental next-fiscal-year revenue, which will be several times the in-year figure. Run the whole thing as a Monte Carlo over the logo delta, land value, implementation lag and close rate, and report the tenth, fiftieth and ninetieth percentiles rather than a point estimate.

The arithmetic to expect, for calibration: a company with two billion dollars of revenue, twelve hundred annual logo adds and a hundred-and-twenty-thousand-dollar average land is adding roughly a hundred and forty million of new-logo ARR per year. A twenty percent acceleration across the two quarters that can still touch this fiscal year is on the order of fourteen million of incremental ARR, of which perhaps a quarter is recognized in-year — about four million, or twenty basis points on revenue. Guidance ranges on a name like this are typically a hundred to two hundred basis points wide. That is the shape of the answer unless the model's calibration says otherwise, and it is why the model must be built before the position, not after.

Run one more module in parallel: how this management team actually sets guidance. Tabulate the last twelve quarters as guided revenue, actual revenue, the beat in dollars, and the change in the full-year midpoint. Compute the flow-through ratio — change in full-year guide divided by the quarter's beat. Most disciplined teams run near 1.0x at the second-quarter print, meaning they pass the beat through and add nothing, and save any genuine raise for the third quarter. If this company's median second-quarter flow-through is 1.0x, then a "meaningful boost" requires the model to produce upside far beyond what new logos can generate, and the burden of proof shifts hard against the thesis.

## Validating it before trusting it

Backtest the whole pipeline on the last eight quarters. For each, reconstruct what the channel commentary said at the time (the notes are archived; deal-registration color and partner tone are usually in them) and ask what the model would have predicted versus what management actually guided. The critical diagnostic is the base rate: if channel checks were characterized as "incrementally positive" in six of the last eight quarters and guidance was meaningfully raised in three, the signal carries essentially no information, and the correct action is to stop. Positive checks are close to the default state of sell-side channel work; that observation alone should be established or refuted before any capital is committed.

Cross-check the implied bookings against current RPO, which is the closest thing to a public bookings disclosure. Take the model's shocked logo count, translate it into implied cRPO growth, and compare with the company's printed cRPO growth over three years. If the implied acceleration would be the highest the company has ever reported, the check is overstated and the credibility weight should come down.

Cross-check against sales capacity. Compute new-logo ARR per ramped quota-carrying rep for the last twelve quarters, where "ramped" means hired more than two quarters ago. If headcount grew eight percent year over year and the model requires a twenty-five percent logo acceleration, it is implicitly assuming a productivity level that should be tested against the historical distribution — anything above the ninetieth percentile of the last three years gets flagged and haircut.

Cross-check against implementation capacity and services revenue. Lands cannot become revenue faster than they can be deployed. If services revenue and implementation headcount are not accelerating a quarter ahead of the claimed bookings surge, the revenue will land in the following fiscal year regardless of what was signed.

Finally, check whether the market already owns this. Look at the stock's move since the notes published, the direction of thirty-day revisions, and the option-implied move versus the eight-print realized average. If the shares have already moved several percent on the checks and implied move is above its trailing average, the edge has been paid away and the remaining trade is a coin flip with negative carry.

## What would go wrong

The most likely error is a category error: treating new logos as the driver of in-year revenue when expansion and churn dominate it. Build the sensitivity table that makes this vivid — for most subscription businesses, a hundred basis points of net retention is worth several times a twenty percent swing in logo count within the fiscal year. If the checks say nothing about the installed base, they say very little about the guide.

Second, mix. A surge in logo count accompanied by a drop in land size — a deliberate down-market push, a product-led motion converting free users, a smaller edition launched at a lower price — can be flat or negative for revenue and negative for unit economics. Always model count and value together, never count alone.

Third, the difference between registered, closed and live. Deal registration inflates when partners prospect harder or when the vendor changes registration incentives; it is a measure of effort as much as of outcome. Ask specifically whether partner economics or registration rules changed this year.

Fourth, guidance is a management decision, not a model output. CFO tenure, a pending investor day, macro caution, a large renewal cohort later in the year, currency, an extra week in the fiscal calendar, a recent acquisition, or a simple desire to preserve room can all override the arithmetic in either direction. Read the last four calls for the exact hedging language and note whether the company has ever raised on the strength of new logos specifically.

Fifth, structural features that break the model: multi-year prepaid contracts that move billings without moving revenue, any term-license component that recognizes in lumps under ASC 606, changes in disclosure (a company that stops reporting customer counts is telling you something), and consumption pricing, which shortens the lag dramatically and would flip the conclusion. Determine the revenue model's true shape before running anything.

Sixth, the reflexivity problem. Everyone read the same notes. If published consensus has already moved to the checked numbers, an in-line-with-checks result is a disappointment, and a stock that has run into the print can fall on a good quarter. Model the reaction against the whisper, not against the printed consensus.

## Turning the output into a position

Do not size until the model has produced a distribution. Map the fiscal-year guidance outcomes onto share price using a simple regression of the last eight prints' post-earnings returns against the change in forward consensus revenue and margin, which gives an empirical dollars-to-percent conversion for this specific stock rather than a guess. Compare the probability-weighted move from that mapping against the option-implied move. The bar: unless the modeled expected move exceeds the implied move by at least 1.2x, the print is not worth owning as an event, and the right expression of a favorable fundamental view is to buy after the print or to define risk with a call spread rather than pay for a straddle-adjacent bet in common stock.

The concrete decision rule: take an event position only if the base case shows fiscal-year revenue running at least 75 basis points above the guidance midpoint and at least 50 basis points above consensus, and only if that implied raise exceeds 1.5x the median second-quarter guidance raise this team has delivered historically, and only if the capacity and cRPO cross-checks both pass. Fail any one of those and the correct answer is that the checks are real, the fundamental direction is favorable, and the print is still not the place to express it.

If the position is taken, size it off the loss, not off the conviction. With a print-day downside scenario of roughly twelve percent and a risk budget of fifty basis points of fund NAV on this event, the position is about four percent of NAV. Set the stop before the print in writing, and make it a thesis stop as well as a price stop. Exit on any of: current RPO growth decelerating year over year; customer adds below the model's base case; a full-year raise at or below simple flow-through of the beat; or a drop in average land value. Price stop at eight percent below entry, and a hard rule to be flat within two sessions after the print regardless of the narrative, because the specific edge being underwritten has by then either paid or expired.

Separately and explicitly, record the twelve-month case, because the model will likely show that the same incremental ARR that is trivial to this year's revenue is meaningful to next year's. If that is what the numbers say, the honest conclusion is a fundamental long built after the event, not through it — and saying so is the most valuable output this work can produce.
