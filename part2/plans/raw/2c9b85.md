## The question, stated so it can fail

The claim under test has two links in it, and both have to hold. First, that trades printed in whole lots of 100 shares are disproportionately retail in origin. Second, that the direction of that flow, aggregated to the stock-day, predicts returns strongly enough and reliably enough that taking the other side earns money after costs. The plan tests the second link directly and uses the first as an identification check rather than an assumption, because the assumption is the weakest part of the story: algorithmic execution engines slice parent orders into 100-share children as a matter of course, and since odd lots entered the consolidated tape in December 2013 the round-lot share of prints has been driven as much by share price as by clientele.

The primary hypothesis: a daily, signed, round-lot order-flow imbalance measured at the stock level predicts negative one-day-ahead returns (retail marketable flow demands liquidity and gets reversed), with the sign attenuating or flipping by twenty days (retail flow contains some information, or at least follows it). The tradeable version of the hypothesis: a long-short book that fades the imbalance earns a positive alpha against a factor set that already includes short-term reversal, and survives a spread-plus-impact cost model at realistic size.

Falsification is explicit. If the round-lot imbalance's incremental Fama-MacBeth coefficient, after controlling for the all-lot imbalance and a subpenny-based retail imbalance, carries |t| < 2.0 at every horizon, the conclusion is that round lots add nothing beyond order flow generally — the lot-size filter is not doing identification work. If the coefficient survives but the net-of-cost long-short alpha has t < 2.0 under the base cost calibration, the conclusion is that the effect is real and unharvestable. Both are publishable answers; neither requires the strategy to work.

## Data

The trade and quote layer is NYSE Daily TAQ via WRDS: the Trades file, the NBBO file, and the Master file for symbol-to-PERMNO linkage. Primary sample is 2 January 2015 through the most recent complete quarter, which starts a year after odd lots became reportable so that the round/odd split is measured consistently throughout. A 2010–2013 extension is run only as a stability check and is reported with the caveat that odd lots are invisible there, which mechanically inflates round-lot share.

Returns, prices, shares outstanding, exchange codes and delisting returns come from CRSP daily. Book equity for the value control comes from Compustat annual, lagged six months. Factor returns come from Ken French's daily files: the five-factor set, momentum, and the daily short-term reversal factor, which is the single most important benchmark here. Securities-lending fees and utilization come from IHS Markit (or Ortex if Markit is unavailable) for the short leg; institutional ownership from Thomson/Refinitiv 13F, quarterly, lagged one quarter. Earnings announcement dates from Compustat and IBES for event masking.

The universe is CRSP share codes 10 and 11 on NYSE, NYSE American and Nasdaq, screened at each month end for price between $5 and $1,000, market capitalization above the NYSE 20th percentile, and twenty-day median dollar volume above $1 million. Stocks are dropped for five trading days after an IPO, for two days around splits and large distributions, and for the day of and day after a scheduled earnings release in the main specification (retained in a robustness cut, since a large part of retail flow is earnings-driven and excluding it may throw away the signal).

## Building the measure

Trade cleaning follows the Holden–Jacobsen conventions. Keep prints between 09:30:00 and 16:00:00 with correction indicator 00 and sale-condition strings composed only of blank, @, F or I; this drops opening and closing auction prints, average-price and derivatively-priced trades, out-of-sequence and extended-hours prints. Drop non-positive prices or sizes, and drop any print more than 10% away from the median of the surrounding twenty prints. The NBBO file is filtered for crossed or locked quotes, non-positive bids, quoted spreads wider than the lesser of $5 and 25% of the midpoint.

Signing uses Lee–Ready against the contemporaneous NBBO — no lag, per the fast-market evidence — with the tick test on the previous different consolidated price for midpoint executions. EMO and CLNV signings are carried as robustness variants; if the headline result moves materially across the three, that is itself a finding about fragility.

Lot classification: odd is size below 100; round is any positive multiple of 100; mixed is above 100 and not a multiple. The primary signal uses round only, with a narrower "retail-plausible round" variant restricted to 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 shares, on the logic that institutional slicers concentrate at 100 while retail dollar-sized orders spread across the small multiples. The 100-share definition is held fixed across the whole sample regardless of the SEC's tiered round-lot redefinition under the Market Data Infrastructure rule; the tiering changes what the tape calls a round lot, not what the measure should mean, and a definition that changes mid-sample would create a break exactly where a regime test wants to look.

The stock-day signal is a dollar-volume imbalance,

RLOIB(i,t) = (buy round-lot dollars − sell round-lot dollars) / (buy round-lot dollars + sell round-lot dollars),

computed only on stock-days with at least twenty round-lot prints and $50,000 of round-lot dollar volume. A second scaling, signed round-lot dollars divided by market capitalization, is carried alongside because the ratio form throws away intensity. Both are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles cross-sectionally and then converted to cross-sectional z-scores each day, so regression coefficients read as return per one-standard-deviation of signal. A smoothed variant is an exponentially weighted average with a three-day half-life over the trailing five days.

## Making the lot filter earn its keep

Three companion signals are constructed identically and enter the same regressions: ALLOIB from every eligible print, ODDOIB from odd lots only, and RETOIB, a subpenny-based retail imbalance computed on off-exchange prints (TAQ exchange code D). For RETOIB, let Z be the fractional cents of the price; Z in (0, 0.4) is classified retail sell and Z in (0.6, 1) retail buy, with prints at exact and half cents discarded. The known misclassification problem in that rule is handled two ways: a stricter variant requires the subpenny sign and the Lee–Ready sign to agree, and a validation exercise compares daily RETOIB against exchange retail-liquidity-program prints and any published venue-level retail volume series over the overlapping window, reporting the correlation rather than assuming it.

The identification test is a horse race. Regress RLOIB on ALLOIB, ODDOIB, log price and log size cross-sectionally each day and keep the residual; that residual is what "round-lot-ness" contributes beyond flow and beyond the mechanical fact that expensive stocks print odd. Then run the forward-return regressions on both the raw and residualized versions. Separately, regress RLOIB on RETOIB stock-by-stock over rolling 250-day windows and report the distribution of R-squared: if round-lot imbalance is a good retail proxy it should track the subpenny measure closely in small, cheap, high-retail names and poorly in mega-caps, and that cross-sectional pattern in fit is a testable prediction rather than a hope.

## Predictive regressions

Signals are computed from day t's tape; positions are assumed established at the close of t+1; returns are cumulated from t+1 to t+1+h for h in {1, 5, 20}. That one-day implementation lag is deliberately conservative and removes any chance of same-close contamination; an intraday-implementation variant (signal from the first five hours, trade at the day-t close) is run as an upper bound on what a faster shop could get.

The cross-sectional regressions each day are

r(i, t+1 → t+1+h) = a + b·RLOIB(i,t) + c'·X(i,t) + e,

with X containing ALLOIB, RETOIB, the day-t return (essential — the imbalance is mechanically correlated with the contemporaneous return, and without this control the coefficient is just reversal in disguise), the prior one-month and 12-minus-2-month returns, log market cap, log book-to-market, twenty-day turnover, Amihud illiquidity, twenty-day realized volatility, log price, the round-lot share of dollar volume, and institutional ownership. Estimation is ordinary least squares on the daily cross-section for the headline, with a weighted variant using square-root-of-market-cap weights on gross returns to blunt the microstructure-noise bias in equal-weighted cross-sections.

Standard errors on the time series of coefficients use Newey–West with h+5 lags for overlapping horizons, cross-checked against Hansen–Hodrick and against a stationary block bootstrap with mean block length twenty days and 5,000 replications. A pooled panel with stock and day fixed effects and standard errors double-clustered by stock and date is reported as a complement, not as the headline, since the question is cross-sectional.

Coefficients are reported in basis points per standard deviation with the implied gross annualized spread between the top and bottom deciles, so the economic magnitude is legible next to the transaction costs that follow.

## Portfolio, costs, and the honest net number

Each day, eligible stocks are sorted into deciles on the signal. The book is long the decile with the most round-lot selling and short the decile with the most round-lot buying — fading the flow — with value weighting inside each leg and a five-day overlapping holding structure (one-fifth of the book turned each day) to cut turnover without changing the horizon being tested. Dollar neutrality is imposed; beta neutrality is imposed in a variant using 250-day rolling betas.

Costs are charged per trade, not assumed away. The spread charge is the stock's twenty-day median dollar-volume-weighted half effective spread from TAQ, applied to every share traded, with Corwin–Schultz high-low spreads as the fallback for days with unusable quote data. Impact is a square-root function, cost in basis points equal to k·σ_daily·sqrt(order size / ADV) with k = 0.5 as the base and 0.25 and 1.0 as stress cases, where order size is set by an assumed AUM and a 5% participation cap. Short-leg financing is charged at the Markit indicative fee; names with DCBS of 5 or worse, or fees above 100 basis points, are excluded from the short leg in the main test and reinstated in a variant so the cost of the exclusion is visible. A flat 1 basis point of commissions and exchange fees is added.

Turnover mitigation follows the buy/hold-band approach: enter on decile 1 or 10, exit only when the name leaves decile 3 or 8. Report gross and net returns and turnover both ways. Report the breakeven round-trip cost that drives alpha to zero, in basis points, next to the measured average round-trip cost for the traded names — that single comparison is the most informative number in the study.

Evaluation is a daily time-series regression of the net long-short return on the market, size, value, profitability, investment, momentum and short-term-reversal factors, plus two in-house controls: a factor formed on the all-lot imbalance (does the round-lot version add anything to plain liquidity provision?) and one formed on the subpenny retail imbalance. Report annualized alpha and its t-statistic, information ratio, turnover, maximum drawdown, worst single day, skewness, and alpha by calendar year. Given the multiplicity involved, the bar for calling it real is t above 3.0 on the net alpha, not 2.0.

## Validation

Split the sample at the end of 2019 and report both halves; the zero-commission transition in October 2019 and the 2020–2021 retail surge make this the natural break, and any signal that lives only in one half is a regime artifact. Report the strategy separately for January–March 2021 — the short leg is by construction short exactly what retail was buying, and that month is the honest stress test of the whole idea.

Run placebos: the same pipeline on mixed lots of 250 to 750 shares, and on a bucket of exact 100-share prints only. If the exact-100 bucket is the weakest of the round-lot variants, that supports the algorithmic-slicing contamination story. Run a timing placebo in which the signal is shifted forward five days; any surviving predictability indicates a bug in the merge. Run a sign-randomization placebo in which trade signs are permuted within stock-day, preserving volume and lot structure, to confirm the imbalance and not the volume is doing the work.

Sensitivity grid, reported as a table of t-statistics rather than a single number: three signing algorithms, two NBBO alignments (contemporaneous and one-millisecond lag), two scalings of the imbalance, raw versus smoothed signal, with and without earnings days, deciles versus quintiles, value versus equal weighting, and three impact calibrations. Robustness on returns: repeat the h = 1 test using close-to-close midquote returns and using t+2 to t+6 returns, both of which strip bid-ask bounce out of the dependent variable — if the one-day result vanishes under midquote returns, it was bounce.

Capacity: recompute net alpha across assumed AUM from $50 million to $5 billion and report the level at which it crosses zero.

## What I expect to go wrong, and how it will be handled

The premise is the main risk. Round lots are not a clean retail marker in the modern tape; institutional child orders cluster at exactly 100 shares, and odd-lot frequency rises sharply with share price, so an unconditional round-lot filter partly sorts on price level. This is why the residualized signal and the price control are in the specification from the start, and why the correlation with the subpenny measure is validated rather than assumed.

The second risk is that the result is short-term reversal wearing a new hat. Order-flow imbalance is mechanically linked to the contemporaneous return; without the day-t return control and the short-term-reversal factor in the alpha regression, a strong result is nearly guaranteed and nearly meaningless. Both are non-negotiable parts of the design.

Third, the sign is expected to differ across horizons, and that is the interesting part rather than a failure. One-day predictability from fading the flow is a liquidity-provision premium. Twenty-day predictability in the same direction would be surprising given the published evidence that retail imbalances positively predict returns at monthly horizons; if the twenty-day coefficient comes out positive, the correct conclusion is that the profitable trade is short-horizon liquidity provision, not "trading against retail," and the writeup should say so plainly.

Fourth, costs will likely dominate. A daily-rebalanced decile spread on small, cheap, high-retail names is exactly where effective spreads are widest, so the gross alpha and the cost line will be the same order of magnitude. That is why the breakeven cost is a headline output.

Fifth, the short leg is structurally exposed to squeeze risk and to borrow scarcity in precisely the names the signal likes. Report the strategy long-only-relative-to-universe as well, and report how much of the alpha lives in the short leg.

Sixth, practical data traps: TAQ symbol changes and the CUSIP-to-PERMNO link across corporate actions; the December 2013 odd-lot break; the tiered round-lot redefinition at the end of the sample; sale conditions that quietly reintroduce auction volume; delisting returns (apply the standard -30% / -55% fills for missing performance-related delists); and look-ahead through quote alignment. Each of these is checked once, in writing, before any return is computed.

Week two: signal construction, the four imbalance measures, and the identification horse race. Week three: Fama-MacBeth regressions and the full sensitivity grid. Week four: portfolios, cost model, factor attribution, capacity, placebos, and the writeup. Deliverables are a reproducible pipeline, one regression table, one portfolio table with gross and net side by side, the sensitivity t-statistic grid, and a two-page memo whose first sentence is the answer.
