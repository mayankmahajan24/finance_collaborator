## Hypothesis

For software companies that charge per named or active seat, the count of distinct humans logging into the vendor's customer-facing web application each month is a direct physical measure of the quantity the vendor invoices for. If seat count is the billing unit, then observed logins should stand in a stable, near-proportional relationship to billed seats, and billed seats times price should be ARR. The testable form is: within a company, a 1% change in monthly active users on its portal is associated with a β% change in ARR one quarter or less later, with β meaningfully positive and stable enough that the residual is smaller than the dispersion of sell-side estimates. The economic claim is not that MAU equals seats — it plainly does not, because of shelfware, shared logins, and tracker coverage — but that the ratio of MAU to paid seats and the average price per seat both move slowly enough within a company that changes in MAU carry the signal.

The null we are trying to reject is that MAU adds nothing beyond what last quarter's ARR and the company's own trend already tell you. That null is strong for this asset class: seat-based SaaS ARR is one of the most autocorrelated series in public equities, and any model with a company intercept will look accurate whether or not the alternative data is doing work. The whole design below is organized around isolating the incremental content of MAU rather than demonstrating that ARR is predictable.

## Universe and data

Start with a universe of 35–60 US-listed software companies whose disclosed pricing is predominantly per-seat and whose primary product is used through a browser at a stable, identifiable hostname. Concretely: collaboration and productivity (Atlassian, Asana, Monday, Smartsheet, Slack-era comparables), CRM and sales tooling (Salesforce, HubSpot, ZoomInfo), design and creative (Adobe's subscription segment, Figma), ITSM and security seats (ServiceNow, CrowdStrike where per-endpoint rather than per-seat should be flagged and probably excluded), HR and finance seats (Workday, Bill.com, Paycom, Paylocity), and communications (Zoom, RingCentral, Twilio's Flex). Exclude anything where the dominant meter is consumption (Snowflake, Datadog, MongoDB, Confluent) or transaction volume (Shopify, Toast), and exclude companies where the portal is an admin console used by a handful of people rather than by every seat. Write the inclusion rule down before looking at any results: seat-metered revenue must be more than 70% of subscription revenue per the 10-K revenue disclosure or management's own characterization, and the product must be primarily consumed in-browser rather than in a thick desktop client or mobile app. Record the exclusions and why; the excluded names become a falsification set later.

For each company, define the portal hostname set explicitly — the authenticated application subdomains (app.\*, \*.my.\*, tenant subdomains under a wildcard) and never the marketing www root, which is dominated by prospects and job seekers. For multi-tenant products with per-customer subdomains, the vendor-level aggregate requires wildcard rollup; confirm the data vendor supports it before committing to a name.

Measurement of MAU comes from a clickstream or device panel that reports distinct users rather than visits: Similarweb's app-level unique-visitor series, a device-graph panel such as Datos or Verto, or a mobile-inclusive source (Sensor Tower, data.ai) where a material share of usage is in an app. Prefer sources that expose a monthly panel-size denominator, because raw uniques from a panel of shifting size are not comparable across time. Where two independent sources exist for the same hostname, keep both and use their disagreement as a data-quality flag.

The dependent variable is a problem worth solving carefully, because most of these companies do not report ARR. Build a hierarchy and record which tier each observation comes from: (1) explicitly disclosed ARR or ACV where management gives it (Atlassian, Asana, Monday, Bill, Samsara-style disclosures); (2) where absent, an implied ARR equal to quarterly subscription revenue annualized, which for ratable revenue is a good approximation up to intra-quarter timing; (3) where subscription revenue is not broken out, total revenue less disclosed professional-services and license revenue. Never mix tiers within a company's own time series — if the disclosure changes, treat it as a break and either splice with an overlap-period ratio or truncate. Pull the actuals from the 8-K press release, not from a vendor's restated field, and stamp each with the release timestamp so the panel is point-in-time.

Consensus comes from Visible Alpha at line-item level (subscription revenue, ARR, net revenue retention, billings) with the pre-print snapshot preserved. Bloomberg BEst is the fallback but is thinner below the revenue line. You need the estimate as of the morning of the print, not the final consensus, and you need the number of contributing analysts and the standard deviation across them.

Everything is quarterly. Align fiscal to calendar by tagging each fiscal quarter with its actual start and end dates and computing MAU over exactly that window, not over a calendar quarter; the fiscal-year offsets at Adobe, Workday, Salesforce, and Atlassian will otherwise inject a systematic one-month misalignment. Aim for at least 20 quarters per company and drop companies with fewer than 12 usable quarters. Realistically that means 2018 or 2019 onward, and roughly 40 companies times 24 quarters, so 900–1,000 usable company-quarters.

## Estimation

Transform both series to natural logs. The specification is log ARR for company i in quarter t regressed on log MAU at horizons h ∈ {0, 1, 2} quarters, with a full set of company indicators and a full set of quarter indicators. Estimate by within transformation (demean by company and by quarter) rather than by dummy variables, and be explicit that the company indicators absorb both the level of panel coverage for that hostname and the company's average revenue per seat, while the quarter indicators absorb the common software-demand cycle, seat-contraction waves during layoff quarters, and any global drift in the tracker's panel size.

The horizon question needs care about what "lag 0" means operationally. A company reporting the March quarter prints in late April or early May, by which time March MAU has been published by the vendor. So the h = 0 specification — contemporaneous quarter MAU against that quarter's ARR — is tradeable and is the specification most likely to work. Horizons of 1 and 2 quarters are genuinely predictive rather than merely coincident and are the interesting result if they hold; they are also the ones where any seat-provisioning lag (users onboard before the contract expands, or after) should show up. Run all three, plus a specification with all three lags entered jointly, and choose on out-of-sample performance rather than in-sample t-statistics. Expect β in the 0.4–0.8 range rather than near 1.0: shelfware and license-versus-login slippage mechanically attenuate the elasticity, and classical measurement error in a panel-based MAU estimate biases the within estimator toward zero, more so the more of the variation the fixed effects have already removed.

Because log ARR is close to a unit-root process with drift and log MAU may be too, the level regression can look excellent and mean nothing. Guard this three ways. First, run a CIPS panel unit-root test on both series and a Westerlund error-correction test for cointegration between them; if they cointegrate, the level specification is defensible and you should also report the error-correction form, where the change in log ARR is regressed on the change in log MAU and the lagged residual from the level relationship. Second, estimate the whole thing a second time in year-over-year log differences, which is the form that actually maps to the surprise you will trade. Third, add a company-specific linear time trend to the level model; if β collapses when company trends are included, the level result was picking up two things drifting upward together, and only the differenced or error-correction result should be traded.

Standard errors: cluster on company, and because quarter effects induce cross-sectional correlation in the residuals, also report Driscoll–Kraay standard errors with a Newey–West lag of 2 to 3 quarters. With roughly 40 clusters, use the wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher weights, 999 replications) for inference on β rather than trusting the asymptotic cluster variance. Report the Pesaran CD test for remaining cross-sectional dependence.

## Validation

Hold out a random 20% of company-quarters, refit on the remainder, and compute mean absolute percentage error on ARR in levels. The stated bar is under 3%, and you will clear it — a company fixed effect plus a quarter fixed effect on a series that grows 20% a year with 2% quarterly noise will get there with the MAU coefficient set to zero. So compute, and lead with, the following instead:

The honest comparison is against three benchmarks fit and evaluated identically. Benchmark A is last quarter's ARR carried forward. Benchmark B is last quarter's ARR grown at the trailing four-quarter average sequential growth rate. Benchmark C is the two-way fixed-effects model with MAU excluded. The claim survives only if the MAU model beats C on out-of-sample error by a margin that is significant under a Diebold–Mariano test on the absolute-error differentials, clustered by quarter, at p < 0.05. Report the ratio of MAPEs, not just the level.

Random holdout of company-quarters leaks: neighboring quarters of the same company are in train and test simultaneously, and the quarter fixed effect for a held-out observation is estimated from its own quarter's other companies. Keep the random split as the headline number since it is the stated target, but gate the decision on a rolling-origin evaluation — train on all data through quarter T, predict every company's quarter T+1, roll forward one quarter, repeat over the last 12 quarters — and on a leave-one-company-out split that tests whether the relationship generalizes to a name whose fixed effect must be estimated from a short burn-in. The rolling-origin errors will be two to four times larger than the random-split errors; that gap is the honest measure of how much the random split flatters you.

The metric that actually matters for trading is not level MAPE. Convert every prediction into an implied year-over-year growth rate and measure error in basis points of growth, then measure the error against consensus in the same units. A model with 2.5% level MAPE on a company growing 20% has roughly ±250 bps of growth error, which is wider than the typical sell-side dispersion on these names and would be untradeable. Set the bar there: median absolute growth error must be below 150 bps and below the cross-analyst standard deviation of consensus for the same company-quarter, in at least 60% of company-quarters.

Also run the falsification set. Fit the same model on the excluded consumption-priced names. If MAU predicts ARR just as well there, the model is picking up a general "software is growing" factor rather than the seat mechanism, and the whole thesis is unsupported.

## Trading

For each covered print, compute predicted ARR (or predicted subscription revenue, mapped through the same relation), subtract consensus, and express the surprise in percent. Trade only when the absolute surprise exceeds 1.5 times the model's rolling-origin RMSE for that company and the model's residual for that company has not been trending (a company whose last three residuals are all the same sign is drifting and should be benched until re-estimated). Enter at the close on the day before the print, exit at the close on the day after, hedge the position with IGV or a sector-neutral basket sized to the name's trailing 60-day beta, and cap any single name at a fixed fraction of gross so that one blown print does not define the quarter. Size positions proportional to surprise divided by the model's residual standard deviation, capped at 2x the base unit.

Track hit rate, average signed return per trade, and — critically — the correlation between the surprise and the actual reported surprise versus consensus, because the model can be right about the print and still lose money when the stock trades on guidance, net revenue retention, or a CFO change. Log every trade with the predicted surprise, actual surprise, and the stock reaction, and after 40 events regress the reaction on the actual surprise to see how much of the payoff is even available.

## Pitfalls

Tracker discontinuities are the single largest threat. Similarweb and comparable vendors periodically re-base their panels and methodology, producing step changes of 10–30% in reported uniques with no underlying change in behavior. A step change in MAU inside a company fixed effect looks exactly like a real seat expansion. Screen every series for structural breaks with a Bai–Perron test on the log level, cross-check any detected break against the vendor's methodology release notes, and either splice with an overlap ratio or drop the pre-break history. Never trade a company within two quarters of a detected break.

Shelfware breaks the mechanism in exactly the situation where you most want the model: an economic downturn where customers stop using seats months before they decline to renew them. MAU will fall and ARR will not, until it does, all at once at renewal. This means the relationship is likely asymmetric — stronger on the way up than the way down — and you should test that explicitly by interacting log MAU with an indicator for negative year-over-year MAU growth. If the downside coefficient is materially smaller, restrict trading to positive-surprise signals or model the two regimes separately.

Price and mix drift silently. Every price increase, every upsell from Standard to Premium, and every shift from monthly to annual contracts raises ARR with no change in login count. A company fixed effect absorbs the level of price but not its changes. Build a manual price-action log per company from pricing-page snapshots on the Wayback Machine and enter a log price index as a control; where you cannot construct one, treat quarters following an announced price increase as flagged and exclude them from trading.

Coverage and composition problems in the panel: enterprise users behind corporate networks and SSO are systematically under-panelled relative to SMB users, so the panel measures a shifting subset; deep links from Slack or email that land inside the app may not be attributed to the app hostname; bot and monitoring traffic inflates counts; and the desktop-versus-mobile split moves as products ship better mobile clients. Where a mobile app exists, either include app MAU or restrict to companies where mobile is immaterial, and hold that choice fixed across the sample.

Corporate actions and disclosure changes: acquisitions add both users and ARR but on different timelines and with restated history; a company that stops disclosing ARR forces a tier change in the dependent variable; fiscal-calendar realignments shift the measurement window. Maintain an event log and treat every one as a mandatory exclusion window of two quarters on either side.

Finally, the research-process risks. The dataset is small — 40 companies is 40 effective clusters, and the horizon choice among 0, 1, and 2 is itself a fitted parameter, so pick it on the rolling-origin split and report the performance of all three so the selection is visible. Vendor data histories are often backfilled and restated, so obtain point-in-time snapshots or explicitly acknowledge that the backtest sees data the trader would not have. And assume this signal is not proprietary: the same panels are sold to everyone, so expect any edge to be concentrated in the smaller-cap, thinly covered names and to decay. Measure the decay by splitting performance by year before you scale capital into it.
