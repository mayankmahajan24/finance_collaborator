## The question, stated so it can be wrong

The observation is that management sounded evasive when analysts pushed on the vendor licence renegotiation. The claim we want to test is not "they sounded evasive" — that is an impression — but this: *when this management team talks about this vendor, they hedge more, answer shorter, give fewer numbers, and dodge more often than they do on every other topic on the same call, and more than they did on this same topic in prior calls; and historically, that specific pattern has preceded gross-margin outcomes worse than the sell side expected.*

That splits into two hypotheses that must both survive.

**H1 (measurement).** The current call's answers on vendor/procurement topics are elevated on an evasiveness score relative to (a) the same speakers' answers on other topics in the same call, and (b) the same speakers' answers on vendor topics in the trailing eight calls. Pre-commit the bar: ≥1.5 standard deviations above the firm's own trailing baseline, where the SD comes from that firm's own call-to-call variation, not the cross-sectional pool.

**H2 (predictive content).** Across a broad panel, that elevation predicts negative gross-margin surprises one to four quarters out, conditional on everything already public. Pre-commit the bar: a 1-SD increase in the within-firm change in the score predicts a gross-margin surprise more negative by ≥15bp with |t| ≥ 2.5 **in a strictly out-of-sample period**, plus a ≥5pp improvement over the base rate in calling the sign of the surprise.

The null we must be willing to publish is that H1 holds and H2 does not — i.e. management is being cagey for reasons (live negotiation, NDA, legal counsel) that carry no information about the eventual cost. That outcome is the *"not a sign of weakening fundamentals"* answer and it is the modal outcome for signals of this kind. Say so up front so nobody treats a null as a failed project.

## Data

Transcripts: S&P Capital IQ or Refinitiv StreetEvents, full text with speaker attribution and turn boundaries, 2005 to present, all US-listed issuers with a market cap above $300m. Speaker metadata matters more than usual — we need role (CEO/CFO/IR/other) and a stable person identifier so we can build per-executive baselines. Drop calls that are translations or where the Q&A is truncated. Pull the *same* 200 calls from a second transcript vendor as a control sample (see validation).

Outcomes: Visible Alpha for line-item consensus on gross margin and COGS (IBES/FactSet EPS consensus is not a substitute — the whole point is margin, and an EPS beat can hide a margin miss). Compustat quarterly for realised revenue, COGS, gross margin, inventory, and the purchase-obligations disclosures scraped from 10-K exhibit tables. IBES for the direction and magnitude of analyst gross-margin revisions in the 60 days after the call. CRSP for CAR(−1,+1) around the *next* print and 3-month post-announcement drift.

Case-specific: the subject company's 10-K purchase-obligations table for the last five years (contractual minimum commitments by year is the hardest evidence there is that a step-up has been agreed), Item 1A supplier-concentration language with year-over-year redlines, the vendor's own filings and public price actions, and whether the subject appears as a >10% customer in the vendor's segment note. Also job postings and capitalised-software trends, which is the cheapest read on whether they are building an in-house replacement.

Lexicons and models: Loughran–McDonald word lists, Brysbaert concreteness norms, spaCy for dependency parsing, one pinned sentence-embedding model, and one pinned LLM version at temperature 0 for turn classification.

## Building the score

**Step 1 — segment.** Discard prepared remarks for the core score; they are drafted and lawyered and carry a different data-generating process (keep them for a separate consistency check on what management *claims* about the vendor). Split Q&A into exchanges: one analyst question plus all management turns until the next analyst speaks.

**Step 2 — tag vendor exchanges.** Two-stage, for precision and recall. First a keyword pass on a curated list: the named vendor(s), plus `renew*`, `renegotiat*`, `licen[cs]e`, `contract terms`, `supplier`, `vendor`, `procurement`, `true-up`, `enterprise agreement`, `seat`, `royalty`, `take-or-pay`, `minimum commitment`, `most-favoured`, `pass through`. Second, embed every exchange and score cosine similarity against ~20 hand-written seed sentences describing supplier-cost renegotiation; take exchanges above a threshold calibrated on 500 hand-labelled exchanges to hit precision ≥0.85 and recall ≥0.75. Store both flags so we can test sensitivity to the tagger.

**Step 3 — measure each management turn.** Five families, each per-100-words unless noted.

*Hedging.* Three sub-counts, reported separately, not merged into one blob: epistemic modals (may, might, could, would, should); approximators (about, roughly, somewhat, relatively, in the range of, a little bit, directionally); and self-distancing evidentials (we believe, we think, our sense is, I would characterise, as I said). Use the dependency parse to strip false positives — "could be a meaningful tailwind" is a forecast, "we would say" is a hedge — by requiring the modal to govern a verb whose subject is the speaker or the company.

*Deflection.* A three-way turn label (direct / partial / deflect) from an LLM classifier, but anchored: hand-label 1,000 turns with two coders, require Cohen's κ ≥ 0.70 between them, then require the classifier to hit ≥0.80 agreement with the consensus human label before it is used. Give the classifier three mechanical features alongside the text: content-word Jaccard overlap between question and answer after lemmatisation and stopword removal (below 0.10 is a strong dodge tell), presence of redirect markers ("stepping back", "the broader point", "we don't disclose", "we're not going to get into", "I'd point you to what we said last quarter"), and whether a question containing a number received an answer containing no number. Run each label three times and take the majority; record self-consistency as a quality metric.

*Specificity.* Numeral density (digits, percentages, dollar figures, dates); named-entity density; count of time-anchored commitments (a future-tense verb with an explicit date or quarter); and mean concreteness of content words from the Brysbaert norms.

*Length, normalised.* Raw answer words, and answer words divided by question words. The normalisation matters — long hostile questions pull long answers, and unnormalised length is mostly a measure of how hard the analyst pushed.

*Interaction.* Whether the analyst immediately follows up on the same topic (a revealed signal that the first answer did not land), how many follow-ups the topic absorbs, and whether the answer is handed from CFO to CEO or bounced to IR mid-topic.

If audio is available, add pre-answer pause length (>1.2s), filled-pause rate, and speech rate. Treat this as an extension, not a dependency; alignment to transcripts is fiddly and the text measures should stand alone.

**Step 4 — baseline and standardise.** This is where the method lives or dies. For each measure, compute the executive's value on vendor turns minus the same executive's value on all other turns in the *same* call. That within-call, cross-topic difference eliminates speaker style, coaching, and the general mood of the quarter. Then take the within-firm change: this quarter's difference minus the mean of the trailing four quarters' differences, scaled by that firm's own trailing SD. Finally, standardise cross-sectionally within quarter and sector so that market-wide language drift (everyone hedged in 2020Q2; everyone hedged about supply chains in 2021) does not masquerade as firm-specific defensiveness.

Require ≥4 prior calls from the same speaker; on executive turnover, fall back to a firm-level baseline and flag the observation as low confidence rather than dropping it.

**Step 5 — combine.** The headline score is the equal-weighted mean of the standardised components, with deflection and specificity entered with the sign that makes "more defensive" positive. Equal weights are the pre-registered primary specification because they cannot overfit. Separately fit an elastic-net logistic on the training fold to learn weights, and require the two to agree in sign and rank-correlate above 0.7 before trusting either. If supervised weights beat equal weights by a lot, that is usually a sign of leakage, not skill.

**Step 6 — quantify uncertainty.** A call may contain three vendor exchanges. Block-bootstrap over exchanges, 2,000 draws, to get a confidence interval on each call's score. Hard rule: fewer than three vendor exchanges or fewer than 150 words of vendor-topic answer, and the score is not estimable — report "insufficient evidence", never a point estimate. Half the failures of work like this come from scoring a single sentence.

## The historical test

Panel of firm-quarters with at least one vendor exchange, roughly 40,000 observations depending on how tight the tagger is. Primary outcome: gross-margin surprise at t+1, defined as realised GM% minus Visible Alpha consensus GM% as of the day before the print; where consensus is thin, substitute realised minus a forecast from a seasonal AR(1) on twelve trailing quarters, and report both versions.

Estimate

  GMsurprise(i, t+1) = α + β·ΔScore(i,t) + γ'X(i,t) + firm FE + quarter FE + ε

with standard errors two-way clustered on firm and quarter, and Driscoll–Kraay as a robustness check since margin shocks are strongly cross-correlated. Controls X: log market cap, GM level and trailing four-quarter change, sector PPI beta as an input-cost proxy, change in inventory-to-sales, revenue surprise at t, the *whole-call* Loughran–McDonald negative-word share (so we isolate topic-specific defensiveness from a generally grim call), question count, vendor-exchange count, question length and question sentiment, and analyst fixed effects.

Secondary outcomes, all pre-specified: cumulative GM change over t+1 to t+4; probability of a downward gross-margin guidance revision within two quarters; sign and size of IBES GM revisions in the 60 days after the call (higher frequency, less noisy, and it tells you whether the sell side heard the same thing you did); and CAR around the t+1 print, which answers the "is this already priced" question that determines whether any of this is actionable.

Also run the portfolio version: decile-sort firm-quarters on ΔScore, sector- and size-neutralise, and report the average GM surprise and post-announcement drift by decile. Report **monotonicity across deciles**, not just top-minus-bottom. A clean spread with a non-monotonic middle is usually two outliers wearing a trenchcoat.

## Validation

Blind human check: 200 exchanges rated 1–5 for evasiveness by coders who cannot see the ticker, the date, or any outcome. Require ρ ≥ 0.6 with the score, or go back and fix the measures.

Placebo topics: build the identical score on FX turns, headcount turns, and buyback turns. If defensiveness about FX predicts margin misses just as well, we have found a general "management under stress" factor — real, but a different and weaker claim than the one we set out to test, and probably already in the price. Report it as such rather than quietly keeping the vendor framing.

Placebo outcome: vendor-topic defensiveness should have little power over *revenue* surprise. If it predicts everything, it is a proxy for something coarse.

Strict out-of-sample: fit on 2005–2016, test 2017–present with zero refitting, and never use any field time-stamped after the call date (consensus vintages, restated Compustat values, and index membership are the three usual leakage sources).

Reverse causality: control for the GM surprise and stock reaction at t. Defensiveness after a bad print is unsurprising; the informative variant is defensiveness on a call where the current print was fine.

Known-outcome falsification: hand-assemble ~40 episodes where a vendor renegotiation demonstrably went badly (a disclosed step-up in minimum commitments, a terminated contract, a subsequent margin guide-down attributed to supplier cost) and ~40 that resolved benignly, and check the score separates them *using only pre-resolution calls*. This is the most persuasive single exhibit you will produce, and it is cheap.

Transcript-source robustness: recompute on the second vendor's version of the overlapping 200 calls. Correlation below 0.9 means the score is partly measuring transcription conventions — filler-word retention and paragraph breaks differ across providers — and the affected components must be dropped.

## Applying it to the subject company

Score the current call and the trailing twelve, plot with bootstrap bands, and report the percentile against both own history and same-quarter sector peers. Then translate: expected GM surprise = β̂ × ΔScore with a prediction interval, carried through to EPS and to a valuation delta, so the output is a number a PM can size rather than an adjective.

Then corroborate outside the text, because the text is a prior, not a verdict. Redline the purchase-obligations table year over year — if the minimum commitments have already stepped up, the answer is in the 10-K and the transcript work is confirmatory. Redline Item 1A supplier language. Check the vendor's disclosed pricing actions and whether the subject is a named >10% customer. Check capitalised software and engineering job postings for an in-house replacement effort. Compare all of this to what management actually said on the call about expected cost impact.

Pre-commit the decision rule now, before seeing results. Call it an under-communicated risk only if (a) ΔScore ≥ 1.5 SD versus own baseline **and** placebo topics are not elevated, (b) the historical β clears the H2 bar out of sample, and (c) at least one non-text corroborant points the same way. If (a) holds without (c), the honest conclusion is "the signal is present, the magnitude is unsupported, size accordingly." If (b) fails, the conclusion is that management's caginess is uninformative — which, given live-negotiation NDAs, is a perfectly sensible thing for it to be.

## What will go wrong

Question complexity is the biggest confound: hard questions produce long hedged answers regardless of what management knows. Controlling for question length, sentiment, numeracy, and analyst identity is not optional.

Cost and contract topics are *inherently* more conditional than other topics — "it depends on volume tiers" is an honest answer, not a dodge. This is precisely why the within-topic, cross-time baseline is the primary specification and the within-call, cross-topic difference is only the first-stage de-styling step.

Legal constraint mimics guilt. A company mid-negotiation genuinely cannot speak. Test this directly by restricting to calls where the renegotiation had already publicly concluded — if the signal survives there, it is about the outcome; if it only exists during live negotiations, it is about lawyers.

Adaptation decays the signal. IR teams read the same research. Estimate β by era and expect meaningful decay after roughly 2018; a signal that only works in 2008–2014 is a history lesson.

Multiple comparisons will manufacture a result if allowed to. Pre-register the primary specification, log every specification run, and report Romano–Wolf stepdown-adjusted p-values on the family.

Effective sample size is far smaller than headline N, because vendor exchanges are rare and clustered in a few sectors. Compute and report effective N and power *before* running the regression, so a null is interpretable rather than embarrassing.

Base rates get lost. State the unconditional frequency of a worse-than-25bp gross-margin miss alongside the conditional frequency in the top score decile. The lift, not the conditional rate, is the finding.

Finally, the failure mode that ends projects like this: a signal that predicts margin misses but not returns. Report both outcomes side by side in every table. If the market already hears the same defensiveness on the same call, the analysis is correct and worthless, and it is better to learn that in week three than in the pitch.
