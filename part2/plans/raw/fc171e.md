# fc171e

## The question restated as a decision

The observable fact is that management became evasive, terse, or unusually scripted when asked about an annual vendor licence renegotiation. The question we are being asked is whether that behaviour is evidence of a margin problem larger than guided. The honest answer is that the behaviour is close to uninformative on its own. Defensive tone around a live commercial negotiation is exactly what a competent CFO produces whether the renewal is going badly or going fine, because anything they say lands on the vendor's desk within a day and becomes the vendor's anchor. Tone here is confounded by the negotiation itself, so it cannot separate the two states of the world we care about.

So the plan sets tone aside deliberately and answers a substitute question that is answerable and that dominates the original for investment purposes: **how much operating margin is at stake per unit of price increase on this contract, and how likely is each unit given the contract's mechanics, the vendor's leverage, and what this vendor has actually extracted from comparable customers and from this customer before?** If the maximum plausible outcome moves operating margin by less than roughly 25 basis points, the tone question is moot and we should stop spending time on it. If the central case moves margin by more than about 75 basis points and is not in guidance, we have a call regardless of what management's voice sounded like.

**Working hypothesis:** the licence is a mid-single-digit share of operating expense, the realistic bad case is a 15–30% uplift phased over the new term, and the resulting operating margin hit is 30–90 bps — material to a quarter, not to the thesis, unless the contract is single-source with a step-function pricing change (e.g. a metric change from per-server to per-core, or a bundling/audit settlement), in which case the tail is several times larger. The plan is designed to falsify the sizing, not to interpret the tone.

## Step 1 — Fix the numerator: what is actually spent on this licence

Nothing else in the plan matters if this number is wrong, and it is almost never disclosed cleanly. Build it from the top down and bottom up and require the two to reconcile within about 20%.

Top-down sources, in order of reliability: the contractual obligations / purchase obligations table in the 10-K (or the equivalent commitments note under IFRS), which frequently breaks out "purchase obligations" by year and sometimes names or footnotes a major software or technology commitment; the concentration-of-suppliers paragraph in the significant accounting policies note, which triggers disclosure at supplier concentration thresholds; the cloud-computing-arrangement disclosures (ASC 350-40 implementation costs, and hosting arrangement fees) which since 2020 have pulled some SaaS spend into a named line; the operating lease and finance lease notes if any of the licence is embedded-lease or hardware-attached; and the "technology and content" / "cost of services" expense lines in the MD&A where management often explains growth as "primarily driven by third-party software and hosting". Pull the last five years so we have a growth trajectory, not a level.

Bottom-up: reconstruct units × price. Get the licensing metric (named users, cores, sockets, nodes, processor value units, consumption units, revenue-based tiering) and the underlying count driver from the company's own operating disclosures — headcount, seat count, transaction volume, compute footprint, store count, whatever the metric keys off. Multiply by public list price from the vendor's published price book or GSA/state-contract schedules, then haircut for the discount tier a customer of this size would sit in. Public-sector pricing schedules (US GSA Advantage, UK Crown Commercial G-Cloud, state master agreements) are the single most underused source here because they publish actual negotiated unit prices by SKU, and they bracket what a large private customer pays.

Cross-checks: vendor 10-K/earnings commentary naming the customer; the customer's own procurement or RFP postings if regulated or public-sector-adjacent; job postings and LinkedIn headcount for the vendor's product administrators inside the customer (a proxy for deployment scale); prior litigation or audit settlements which sometimes disclose contract value.

Outputs required before proceeding: annual licence spend in dollars, its split between cost of revenue and operating expense, licence spend as % of COGS, as % of total opex, as % of revenue, and as % of EBIT. Also the capitalised versus expensed split, because a term licence with a material upfront element under ASC 985-20 / 350-40 hits the P&L on an amortisation schedule and lags cash by quarters.

## Step 2 — Contract mechanics, because timing decides how much lands in the guided period

Establish: the renewal date and term length (1 / 3 / 5 year); whether the renewal is evergreen with auto-renew or requires affirmative re-signature; the existence and level of a price-protection cap (typically CPI-linked, or a stated 3–5% annual ceiling) and whether that cap survives into the new term or only applied inside the expiring one; true-up and true-down rights, and whether the customer can shrink the entitlement or only grow it; whether the metric is being changed by the vendor at renewal (this is the single most important line item — metric changes are where 40%+ effective uplifts hide while headline unit price looks flat); audit clauses and any open audit; termination-for-convenience rights and notice windows; most-favoured-customer clauses; and whether support/maintenance is separately priced at the usual 20–22% of licence value, since support uplifts are where vendors take price when new-licence price is capped.

Sources: the material-contracts exhibit list (Item 15 / Exhibit 10.x) — large licence agreements are sometimes filed with redactions; the risk factors in Item 1A, which often describe the dependency and occasionally the renewal timing; the vendor's standard master agreement and current price list, publicly posted for most large enterprise software vendors; and 8-Ks around prior renewal dates.

## Step 3 — The vendor's revealed pricing behaviour toward comparable customers

We want evidence, not adjectives about "market power". Concretely: the vendor's disclosed price-list changes over the last three years (most publish effective dates and percentages); its reported net revenue retention or dollar-based expansion rate, which is the cleanest single public statistic on realised uplift across its base — an NRR of 118% with flat seat growth is a price story; its ASC 606 remaining performance obligation growth versus billings growth, which separates volume from price; and management commentary on "pricing actions", "list price adjustments", and "packaging changes" on its own earnings calls. Then triangulate with third-party benchmarks: Gartner and Forrester licence-negotiation notes, the software asset management advisory firms that publish observed uplift distributions by vendor, and public-sector contract award histories where the same SKU is repriced on a visible schedule.

Assemble a comparable set of five to ten customers of similar size and deployment shape that renewed with this vendor in the last 24 months, and record the observed uplift where it can be established. The output is a distribution — median, 75th, 90th percentile uplift — not a point estimate.

Switching cost gets sized, not asserted: estimate the migration programme cost as (integration points × implementation cost per point) plus retraining plus parallel-run licensing for the overlap period, and compare it to the NPV of the price delta over the new term. If migration NPV exceeds three years of the price increase, the customer's threat to leave is not credible and we should assume the vendor knows it and prices near the top of its comparable distribution.

## Step 4 — Anchor on this specific pair's history

Prior outcomes between these two counterparties are the highest-signal prior available. Reconstruct the last two or three renewals: date, term, and the observed inflection in the relevant expense line in the four quarters following each. Read the earnings-call transcripts from the quarter of and the quarter after each prior renewal and record both what management said in advance and what actually showed up in the numbers — this gives us management's historical accuracy on this exact issue, which is far more useful than their tone this time. If management previously said "no material change" and the line then stepped up 18%, that is a calibrated reason to discount current reassurance; if they have been accurate three times running, the current defensiveness is probably just negotiation hygiene.

## Step 5 — The sensitivity table, which is the deliverable

Build a single table with price uplift on one axis and, on the other, the phasing assumption (immediate full-year effect; renewal mid-year so half-year effect in the guided year; three-year ramp with an equal-thirds step). Cells contain: incremental dollar cost, basis points of operating margin, EPS impact, and impact on the consensus number for the guided year.

The core arithmetic, stated once so it is auditable: margin impact in bps = (licence spend × uplift × share landing in the period × (1 − share capitalised in the period)) ÷ revenue × 10,000. EPS impact = that dollar amount × (1 − marginal tax rate) ÷ diluted shares. Run the uplift axis at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, and 50%, and add one structural scenario for a metric change, modelled as a change in billable unit count rather than unit price, because that is how it actually behaves.

Then invert it. The decision-useful number is the breakeven: what uplift is required to (a) put the company below the low end of its own guided operating margin range, (b) miss consensus EPS by 2%, and (c) breach any covenant headroom on a leverage or fixed-charge test. Express those three as required uplift percentages and then ask where each sits in the comparable-customer distribution from Step 3. If a consensus-breaking outcome requires a 45% uplift and the vendor's 90th percentile observed uplift is 22%, we are done — the risk is not there, and the defensive tone is noise.

Also compute the offsets, because a one-sided table overstates risk: contracted price escalators the company can pass through to its own customers, the licence-related headcount or infrastructure savings the vendor's new packaging often bundles in, and any capitalisation shift that delays P&L recognition.

## Validation

Three tests before the table is trusted. First, historical back-test: run the model backwards on the prior renewal with the then-known inputs and check whether it would have predicted the realised expense-line move within the model's own error bars; if it would not have, the elasticity or the base spend is wrong. Second, reconciliation: the bottom-up units × price estimate and the top-down disclosed-commitments estimate must agree within 20%, and if they do not, the gap must be explained (usually multi-year prepayment, or spend sitting in capex) rather than averaged away. Third, adversarial review: have someone argue the position that the licence is half the size we think, and see whether any disclosure actually contradicts them. Additionally, sanity-check the total: if the implied licence spend exceeds roughly 15% of the company's entire technology cost base, the estimate is probably capturing a bundle rather than one contract.

## Pitfalls to watch

Anchoring on the tone anyway — the entire discipline of this plan is that the tone observation earns no weight in the model, and it will keep trying to sneak in as an upward nudge to the "likely" uplift; do not let it.

Confusing gross uplift with net P&L effect. Headline uplift percentages quoted in trade press are usually list-to-list, while the company negotiates off a discounted base; a 30% list increase against a customer whose discount deepens from 55% to 62% is roughly a 15% realised increase.

Missing the metric change. A vendor holding unit price flat while redefining the counted unit is the standard playbook and it does not show up as a price increase anywhere in the disclosures.

Timing errors that make a real cost look like a fake one, or vice versa. Cash goes out on signature, expense recognises over the term, and capitalised implementation costs amortise over years. Guidance is set on the expense, so a large cash outflow with no margin effect is not a margin story.

Double-counting against guidance. If management has already embedded an expected uplift in the guided opex growth, the incremental surprise is only the delta above that embedded assumption; the honest comparison is our central case against their embedded case, not against zero.

Survivorship in the comparable set. Customers who negotiated badly do not publicise it, so observed public uplifts are biased low; treat the comparable distribution's 75th percentile as the median for a customer with high switching costs.

Over-reading a single quarter. Renewal effects land unevenly and a step-up can be masked for two quarters by a prepaid balance unwinding; require four quarters of post-renewal data before declaring the model right or wrong.

And the largest one: concluding that because the exposure is small, nothing is wrong. Sizing the licence answers only the licence question. If the table shows the contract cannot matter and management is still visibly uncomfortable, that is a genuine signal that the discomfort is about something else, and it should be logged as an open question for separate work rather than resolved by this analysis.
