# b58702

## The question, sharpened

Within an industry, when the market marks up its expectation of a company's next-two-year revenue and EBITDA growth, does that company's forward EV/EBITDA expand relative to its peers — and by how much per point of growth?

The economic content is not "do growth and multiples correlate" (they obviously do in levels, and that correlation is contaminated by everything from capital intensity to accounting). It is whether *changes* in expected growth are priced *within* the peer set, contemporaneously, and at what exchange rate. That exchange rate is the number a fundamental analyst actually needs: if I believe consensus is 200bp light on a company's two-year EBITDA CAGR, how many turns of EV/EBITDA is that worth if I am right and the market comes to agree?

## Hypothesis

**H1 (main).** Within a GICS sub-industry and quarter, a one-percentage-point increase in the consensus next-two-year EBITDA CAGR is associated with a positive change in log forward EV/EBITDA. My prior, from the arithmetic of a two-stage DCF at an 8.5% WACC, is that a *durable* 100bp of growth is worth roughly 8–12% on the multiple. My prior for what we will *measure* is materially less — call it 2–5% per 100bp — because most consensus revisions are treated by the market as partly transitory, and because of the mechanical drag discussed below. The gap between the theoretical and estimated elasticity is itself the interesting finding: it measures how much durability the market assigns to a revision.

**H2.** Revenue CAGR revisions and EBITDA CAGR revisions are not interchangeable. Conditional on the EBITDA revision, the revenue revision should carry additional positive weight (revenue-led growth is read as more durable than margin-led growth). If the incremental coefficient on revenue is zero or negative once EBITDA is controlled for, the market is treating the source of growth as irrelevant, which would be a genuine surprise worth writing up.

**H3.** The relationship is stronger when within-industry dispersion in growth revisions is high. When peers are all revised together, the revision is industry news and is absorbed by the fixed effect; when peers separate, the revision is company-specific information and is priced more aggressively. Operationally: the interaction of ΔCAGR with standardized within-industry-quarter dispersion of ΔCAGR is positive.

**H4.** Elasticities differ systematically by industry, and are largest where multiples are most growth-sensitive by construction — software, semis, life-science tools, medtech — and smallest in capital-intensive, asset-backed, or heavily regulated industries — utilities, integrated energy, telecoms, real estate — where multiples are anchored to asset value and cash return rather than growth.

## Data required

**Universe.** Russell 3000 plus developed-market non-US large and mid caps (MSCI World constituents), 2005Q1 through the most recent complete quarter. Point-in-time constituency, so that firms enter and exit as they actually did — no current-membership backfill. Include dead tickers (acquired, delisted, bankrupt); their exclusion is the single most common source of an inflated growth-pays-off result.

**Consensus estimates.** I/B/E/S Detail or Refinitiv/LSEG consensus for revenue and EBITDA, snapshotted monthly at each month-end, point-in-time (the "unadjusted" summary tape, or Detail rebuilt from individual analyst records) so that later restatements and split-adjustments do not leak backward. If Visible Alpha is available, prefer it for EBITDA specifically — I/B/E/S EBITDA coverage is thinner and more definitionally heterogeneous than EPS. Required per firm-month: FY1, FY2, FY3 revenue and EBITDA means, number of contributing estimates, standard deviation of estimates, and the fiscal period end dates.

**Prices, EV components, fundamentals.** Daily prices and shares outstanding (point-in-time, with a check that share counts update on secondaries and buybacks rather than only at fiscal year-end); most recently *reported-as-of-date* net debt, minority interest, preferred, and pension deficit from Compustat/Worldscope with the actual filing date attached so nothing is used before it was public. Trailing twelve-month revenue and EBITDA. Sector: GICS at the sub-industry (8-digit) level with point-in-time history, since GICS has been restructured several times and current codes misclassify the past.

**Auxiliaries.** 10-year yields (US, EUR, JPY) and breakevens; a within-industry return-dispersion series; and a flag for the IFRS 16 / ASC 842 lease-accounting transition (fiscal 2019), which mechanically shifted reported and estimated EBITDA and lease-adjusted EV for a large slice of the universe.

## Constructing the two key variables

**Forward multiple.** Do not use raw FY1. Use a *constant-horizon* next-twelve-month EBITDA built by time-weighting FY1 and FY2 by the fraction of the next twelve months falling in each fiscal year. This removes the sawtooth that otherwise appears every fiscal-year roll and that would otherwise show up as a spurious quarterly "re-rating." EV is measured at the same month-end as the estimate snapshot, using the latest *publicly filed* balance sheet. Multiple is EV / NTM EBITDA; the dependent variable is Δ log of that, quarter over quarter, using month-end snapshots at calendar quarter ends.

**Growth variable.** Similarly time-weight to build NTM and N2Y (next-24-month) revenue and EBITDA, then define CAGR = (N2Y-implied year-2 level / NTM level) − 1, i.e. the second-year growth rate implied by consensus, expressed in percentage points. Doing it this way — rather than FY0→FY2 — keeps the horizon fixed and keeps the base out of reported history, which matters because reported history is itself revised. ΔCAGR is the quarter-over-quarter change in that number, in percentage points.

**Screens.** Require NTM EBITDA > 0 and N2Y EBITDA > 0; at least 4 contributors to FY2 EBITDA; market cap above $500m in 2024 dollars; EV/NTM EBITDA between 2× and 60× (outside that band the ratio is not measuring what we think it is); and at least 8 qualifying firms in the sub-industry-quarter, otherwise roll the peer group up to the GICS industry (6-digit) level, and drop if still under 8. Winsorize ΔCAGR and Δ log multiple at the 1st and 99th percentiles *within industry-quarter*, not pooled, so the winsorization does not silently absorb the cross-sectional variation we are trying to use.

## The estimator

Core specification, firm *i*, sub-industry *s*, quarter *t*:

Δ log(EV/EBITDA_NTM)ᵢₜ = β₁·ΔCAGR^EBITDA + β₂·ΔCAGR^Rev + γ′Xᵢₜ + δₛₜ + εᵢₜ

with δₛₜ a full set of sub-industry × quarter fixed effects (roughly 150 sub-industries × 80 quarters, absorbed via high-dimensional fixed-effects demeaning — `reghdfe` or `pyfixest.feols`, not dummy expansion). Every coefficient is therefore identified purely from how a firm moved relative to its own peers in that quarter. Common shocks — rates, the market multiple, sector rotation, oil, the whole macro block — are differenced out by construction, which is exactly why this design is worth the effort and why no macro controls are needed.

**Controls X.** Log market cap at t−1; net debt / NTM EBITDA at t−1 and its change (levered equities re-rate more for a given EBITDA change, and this is a confound not a channel); NTM EBITDA margin level at t−1 and Δmargin; lagged 12-month total return (momentum, to absorb drift); Δ log NTM EBITDA itself (see the mechanical-bias note below); analyst count and Δanalyst count; and the coefficient of variation of FY2 EBITDA estimates (estimate dispersion at the firm level, distinct from peer dispersion) plus its change.

**Standard errors.** Two-way clustered on firm and on quarter. Quarter clustering is essential: even with industry-quarter fixed effects, residual within-quarter correlation across industries is large, and firm-only clustering will roughly halve the standard errors. As a robustness check, Driscoll–Kraay with 8 quarterly lags. Report both; if they diverge materially, trust the more conservative.

**Weighting.** Equal-weight as the primary, since the question is about the cross-sectional relation and equal weighting is the honest average firm. Re-run weighted by square-root of market cap; if the elasticity is much smaller in the cap-weighted version, the effect lives in small caps and that belongs prominently in the write-up.

**Scaling.** Report β₁ two ways: as a percent change in multiple per 100bp of CAGR, and as *turns* per 100bp evaluated at the industry's median multiple (β₁ × median EV/EBITDA). Portfolio managers think in turns.

## The mechanical-bias problem and how to handle it

This is the specification issue that decides whether the study is credible. The dependent variable is Δ log EV − Δ log EBITDA_NTM. The regressor is built from the same estimate tape. If FY2 EBITDA is revised up and FY1 is not, CAGR rises with no denominator effect — fine. But if the whole curve shifts up, NTM EBITDA rises, and with EV sticky within the day the multiple falls *mechanically*, biasing β₁ toward zero or negative. The single-equation result would understate the true re-rating.

Handle it by decomposition rather than by hoping. Run the identity in three pieces against the same right-hand side: (a) Δ log EV, (b) Δ log EBITDA_NTM, (c) their difference, the multiple. Coefficients must satisfy β_EV = β_EBITDA + β_multiple exactly — a useful arithmetic check on the pipeline. The headline economic number is β on Δ log EV: *this is what the enterprise is now worth*, and it is immune to the mechanical issue. The multiple regression then tells you how that value change splits between "earning more" and "being paid more per unit." Additionally, always include Δ log NTM EBITDA as a control in the multiple regression, which makes β₁ the effect of a *shape* change in the estimate curve (steeper, same near-term level) — the cleanest reading of "growth re-rating." Report the elasticity both with and without that control and explain the difference rather than picking the flattering one.

## Dispersion test

For each sub-industry-quarter compute D_st = cross-sectional standard deviation of ΔCAGR^EBITDA across the peers, then standardize D within sub-industry across time (so we are comparing an industry to its own normal, not semis to utilities). Estimate

Δ log multiple = β₁·ΔCAGR + β₃·(ΔCAGR × D̃ₛₜ) + controls + δₛₜ

Note D̃ enters only interacted; its level is collinear with the fixed effect. H3 predicts β₃ > 0. Complement with a hard split — top vs bottom tercile of D̃ — estimated separately, so the reader sees two elasticities rather than an interaction coefficient. Then repeat with two alternative dispersion proxies to check the result is about dispersion and not about volatility regimes generally: within-industry *return* dispersion, and the aggregate share of firms with |ΔCAGR| > 200bp. If the effect only shows up for the return-dispersion proxy, we have found a volatility effect, not an information effect, and should say so.

## Industry-level elasticities

Interact ΔCAGR with a full set of GICS-industry (6-digit, ~70 groups; or 24 industry groups if precision is thin) dummies within a single pooled regression, keeping the sub-industry-quarter fixed effects. Raw per-industry slopes will be noisy, so shrink them empirical-Bayes toward the pooled mean using w_i = τ²/(τ² + σ̂ᵢ²), with τ² estimated from the between-industry variance of the raw slopes net of average sampling variance. Report raw and shrunk side by side with 95% intervals. Rank-order stability is the test that matters: split the sample at 2015 and check the Spearman correlation of industry rankings across halves. If that correlation is below ~0.4, the industry heterogeneity is noise and the honest conclusion is a single pooled elasticity.

## Validation

**Falsification via placebo peer groups.** Re-assign each firm to a random "industry" of the same size and re-estimate. β₁ should collapse toward the level explained by common market movement. Run 500 draws and report the placebo distribution alongside the true estimate.

**Timing / reverse causality.** The contemporaneous regression cannot separate "estimates moved and the multiple followed" from "the stock moved and analysts followed." Two checks. First, re-run at monthly frequency with the estimate revision measured over month *t* and the multiple change over month *t+1* — the predictive version. A large predictive coefficient means we have found an anomaly, not a re-rating relation, and that is a different (more interesting, more suspicious) paper. Second, restrict to revisions occurring in the 5 trading days *after* an earnings release, where the causal ordering (news → estimates → price) is cleanest, and compare β₁ to the full sample.

**Pre-trend.** Include ΔCAGR at t+1 as an additional regressor. It should be small and insignificant. If future revisions predict today's multiple change, the estimate tape is stale relative to prices and the whole timing structure needs to move.

**Robustness grid, all reported in one table.** Alternative dependent variables (EV/Sales, forward P/E, EV/EBIT); alternative horizons (one-year and three-year CAGR); FY-based rather than time-weighted construction; sub-industry vs industry-group fixed effects; excluding 2008Q3–2009Q2 and 2020Q1–2020Q2; excluding fiscal 2019 for IFRS 16; US-only vs ex-US; and dropping firms with fewer than 8 estimate contributors.

**Economic validation.** Compare the estimated β₁ to the theoretical elasticity of EV/EBITDA to a permanent change in second-stage growth from a simple three-stage model calibrated per industry (WACC from industry beta, terminal growth 2.5%, industry-median cash conversion). If measured β₁ is roughly 25–50% of theoretical, that is a coherent story of partial durability. If it exceeds theoretical, something in the pipeline is wrong.

**Practical validation.** Each quarter, sort firms within industry into quintiles by ΔCAGR and track the subsequent one-quarter change in relative multiple out of sample, using an expanding window from 2012. Report the spread and its t-statistic. This is the version a PM will believe.

## Pitfalls I will actively watch for

Restated and backfilled consensus data is the top risk — I/B/E/S adjusted-tape rounding for split-adjusted per-share items has generated famous phantom results, and though we use aggregate revenue and EBITDA rather than per-share EPS, the same point-in-time discipline applies. Second, EBITDA definitional drift: I/B/E/S EBITDA is a contributor-defined consensus, and firms migrate between "reported," "adjusted," and "pre-SBC" definitions; a definitional switch shows up as a huge fake ΔCAGR. Flag and drop firm-quarters where |ΔCAGR| > 20pp with no corresponding earnings event or price move above 5%. Third, the IFRS 16 lease transition, which changed both EBITDA and (if lease liabilities are picked up in net debt) EV for retail, restaurants, airlines and shipping in the same year — treat 2019 as a data break for those industries. Fourth, small-denominator instability: firms near zero EBITDA generate enormous CAGRs and enormous multiples; the 2×–60× band plus positive-EBITDA screen handles most of it, but check that the estimated β₁ is not being driven by the bottom margin decile by re-running excluding it. Fifth, stale consensus for thinly covered names — with 4 analysts a single contributor's update moves the mean several hundred basis points, which is measurement error that attenuates β₁; the analyst-count robustness split diagnoses this directly. Sixth, survivorship, in both directions — dropping acquirees biases the growth-pays result upward, since high-growth names get taken out at premium multiples. Seventh, mistaking a fixed effect for identification: with sub-industry-quarter effects, industries with 8–10 members yield within-group variation dominated by one or two firms, so report the distribution of effective group sizes and confirm the result survives a minimum-15-firm restriction. Eighth, the dispersion test is endogenous — dispersion is high precisely when there is a lot of company-specific news, so a positive β₃ may partly reflect news volume rather than a change in how growth is priced; controlling for the count of earnings events and guidance changes in the industry-quarter is the partial fix, and the residual ambiguity should be stated rather than papered over.

## Deliverables and sequence

Week 1: data assembly and the arithmetic identity check (β_EV = β_EBITDA + β_multiple to within numerical tolerance) — nothing else runs until that holds. Week 2: pooled elasticity with the full control set, decomposition table, and the placebo-peer distribution. Week 3: industry elasticities with shrinkage and the dispersion interaction. Week 4: robustness grid, out-of-sample quintile test, and a two-page note whose headline is a single table: turns of EV/EBITDA per 100bp of two-year EBITDA CAGR, by industry, with intervals — plus an explicit statement of what the number is *not* (it is a within-industry, contemporaneous re-rating rate, not a trading signal).
