# e05d0b

## The question, restated as something testable

Management has told the market that it cannot make enough product to meet demand, and that the condition persists through next year. The market's reflex is to read that as a durability signal: scarce supply forces customers to lock in, locking in shows up as long-term agreements, and long-term agreements convert an inherently cyclical revenue line into something closer to an annuity. The plan below is built to determine whether that chain actually holds for this company, and to price it.

The hypothesis I want to test, stated so it can fail: shortage guidance causes a measurable step-up in contractually committed forward volume, and the committed volume converts to revenue at a high enough rate, over a long enough horizon, that forward revenue variance falls materially relative to the company's own history — and the current enterprise value does not already assume that.

My prior, before touching the data, is that the first link is real and the second is weak. Shortages reliably produce agreement announcements, because both sides want them: the customer wants allocation, the supplier wants a document to show investors and a justification for capex. What the shortage does not reliably produce is enforceable, cash-supported, price-fixed commitment. The typical shortage-era agreement is an option held by the customer, dressed as an obligation. The analytical work is separating the two, and the model below is essentially a machine for doing that separation and then pricing the residue.

## What I build

Everything rests on one object: a quarterly forward revenue bridge, extending eight quarters past the guided shortage-resolution date, in which every dollar of projected revenue is tagged with the strength of the claim behind it.

Start with the roll-forward, which is the discipline that keeps the rest honest. For each quarter, remaining performance obligations at the end equal RPO at the start, plus gross bookings, minus revenue recognized, minus cancellations and scope reductions, plus or minus currency and acquisition effects. Companies disclose RPO and revenue; they almost never disclose gross bookings and cancellations separately. So I solve for net bookings as a plug and then, using any disclosed book-to-bill or bookings commentary from the call, back into implied cancellations. The diagnostic that matters: if implied gross bookings would have to be negative to reconcile the disclosed numbers, the company is quietly absorbing cancellations while describing backlog as firm. That single derived series has, in past cycles, turned before the reported backlog did.

Layer three distinct concepts that companies deliberately blur, and never let them merge in the model. First, RPO under ASC 606-10-50-13, from the revenue recognition footnote, split into the twelve-month bucket and the remainder — this is audited and requires enforceable rights and obligations. Second, "backlog," an Item 101 disclosure with no accounting definition, which the company controls and can redefine. Third, the announced long-term agreement value, which typically appears only in IR decks and prepared remarks and appears in no filing at all. The gap between the announced LTA number and RPO is the most informative number in this entire exercise. If the company touts several billion dollars of long-term agreements and RPO barely moves, the agreements lack enforceable minimum quantities or fixed pricing — that is precisely why they fail the RPO test. I want that gap measured every quarter and charted against the narrative.

Then build the agreement register: one row per announced arrangement, sourced from 8-K exhibits, earnings decks, prepared remarks, customer press releases, and the commitments and contingencies footnote. For each, record counterparty, headline value, term, start date, ramp schedule, product line, and then six structural attributes that determine whether it is worth anything. Does it specify minimum quantities, or only "expected" volumes and best efforts? Is price fixed, indexed to a cost input, or subject to periodic renegotiation? Is there customer cash — a prepayment, a capacity reservation fee, tooling or capex funding? That cash should be visible as contract liabilities or customer deposits on the balance sheet and in operating or financing cash flow; if a company claims prepaid capacity commitments and the balance sheet does not move, the claim is soft. Is there take-or-pay with stated liquidated damages, and what percentage of the shortfall? Is it non-cancelable and non-reschedulable, or merely non-cancelable within a lead-time window that the customer can stretch by pushing out delivery dates? And finally, is it included in RPO — and if not, what does management say when asked?

From those attributes, tier every agreement and apply a realization haircut. Tier 1 — customer cash on the balance sheet, take-or-pay with real damages, included in RPO — carries roughly 90 to 95 percent expected conversion. Tier 2 — binding minimums, in RPO, no cash support — I would start near 70 percent. Tier 3 — memoranda of understanding, "strategic partnerships," design wins framed as commitments, anything outside RPO — 25 to 35 percent, and in a downturn scenario closer to zero. Critically, I do not want those haircuts to stay assumptions. I calibrate them from this company's and its peers' own prior episode: take the peak non-cancelable backlog figure from the last shortage, follow it forward eight quarters, and measure what fraction actually became revenue versus what was cancelled, repriced, or quietly pushed beyond the horizon. The 2021–2023 semiconductor episode is the richest calibration set, because several companies published committed-backlog and long-term-agreement dollar values at the peak and then had to explain their disappearance. Where a company's own history gives a realization rate below my tier default, I use the company's history.

Coverage is then computed properly. For each forward quarter, coverage equals haircut-adjusted committed revenue scheduled for that quarter, divided by modeled revenue for that quarter. Two mechanics matter here and are where most versions of this analysis go wrong. First, annualize: a five-year, five-billion-dollar agreement is a billion a year, and against a four-billion run rate that is 25 percent coverage, not 125 percent. Convert every headline to an annual rate against the correct segment's revenue, never against total company revenue, and never against a single year. Second, decompose committed value into price and quantity. Shortage-era agreements are usually struck at shortage prices. If the agreement fixes volume but not price, the durable component is the volume, and revenue durability is only as good as the price path — so I model quantity coverage and revenue coverage separately, and the spread between them is a stated risk, not a rounding error.

## Segmentation

Run the coverage calculation by product line and by customer, not in aggregate, because aggregate coverage hides the two failure modes that actually kill the thesis.

By product line: coverage concentrated in the product that is genuinely capacity-constrained is a different asset than coverage spread evenly, which usually signals customers hedging across a catalog. I want to know whether the constrained product is constrained because of a hard physical bottleneck — a specific substrate, tool, furnace, qualification, or single upstream supplier — or because the company chose not to add capacity. Hard bottlenecks that competitors also face support durability; discretionary tightness invites competitor capacity and prices the durability away within the LTA term.

By customer: pull the ten-percent-customer disclosure from the segment footnote and top-customer language from the risk factors, and map agreement counterparties onto them. Compute a concentration index on the agreement book itself. The asymmetry to look for is correlated failure — agreements signed by customers who are themselves riding the same shortage-driven boom. When the cycle turns, the customer's demand collapse and the agreement's renegotiation arrive in the same quarter. An agreement book concentrated in three counterparties in one end market is not diversifiable visibility, whatever its stated term.

The single best external validation is the mirror disclosure. Public customers must report purchase obligations in their own contractual-obligations tables and commitments footnotes. If this company claims a given dollar value of customer commitments, some material fraction should be visible on the other side of the trade. Sum what the identifiable public customers disclose. A recovery rate well below half is strong evidence the commitments are not binding obligations in the customers' own accounting judgment — and their auditors have seen the same contracts.

## Scenarios and stress

Model three shortage-resolution dates: two quarters earlier than guided, on the guided date, and four quarters later. Attach to each a full path, not a growth rate.

Early resolution is the dangerous case and deserves the most work. It implies competitor capacity or demand normalization arriving before the agreement book matures, and it triggers three effects simultaneously: pricing rolls off toward pre-shortage levels, customers exercise the pushout flexibility embedded in delivery scheduling, and channel inventory built during the panic unwinds. Model the inventory air pocket explicitly — estimate normal days of inventory at distributors and at the identifiable direct customers from their own balance sheets, measure the excess above normal, and let sell-in undershoot sell-through until the excess drains. That undershoot, not the demand level, is what produces the violent revenue quarters in these episodes.

Late resolution is not simply the good case. It extends pricing but also extends the incentive for customers to qualify a second source, and it means the capacity the company is currently building lands into a market that has already been structurally re-sourced. In every scenario, carry the capex commitments forward into a depreciation schedule and let that fixed cost persist past the agreement term. Capacity added against a three-year agreement depreciates over ten to fifteen years. The margin trough that creates is a core output of the model, and it is the reason revenue durability and earnings durability can diverge sharply.

## Connecting to the market price

Two comparisons, one simple and one structural.

The simple one: compute enterprise value divided by haircut-adjusted committed gross profit — committed revenue at a normalized, not peak, gross margin — and run it for the company today, for the same company at the prior cycle peak and trough, and across the peer set. This has the virtue of being hard to argue with and immediately comparable.

The structural one: a reverse discounted cash flow that solves for the number of forward years of covered revenue, at mid-cycle margins, required to justify today's enterprise value. Call that the market-implied visibility horizon. Compare it directly to the modeled durable horizon coming out of the coverage work. If the model supports four years of high-confidence coverage and the price implies six, the shortage narrative is already more than absorbed and the asymmetry is negative regardless of how good next quarter looks. That comparison is the actual output of this project.

## Validation

Backtest the question itself rather than assuming its premise. Assemble every episode over the last two decades where a company guided to supply shortage persisting beyond the current year — memory and passive components in 2017 to 2018, semiconductors broadly in 2021 to 2022, electrical equipment, transformers, turbines and high-bandwidth memory in the recent AI-driven cycle, and further back, telecom equipment in 2000. For each, record the date of the guidance, the count and value of agreements announced in the following four quarters, the RPO and backlog trajectory, realized revenue against what coverage implied at the time, and forward returns at one, two and three years. This produces a base rate: how often shortage guidance was followed by durable revenue rather than by an air pocket, and — more usefully — what distinguished the cases that held. My expectation is that the discriminating variables will be customer cash support and whether the bottleneck was physical, not the headline size of the agreement book.

Second validation track: coherence checks on the company's current numbers. Rising backlog alongside rising finished-goods inventory at the supplier is contradictory and usually resolves in favor of the inventory. Lengthening days sales outstanding during a claimed shortage means the company is financing customers who supposedly have no alternative. Revenue recognized from capacity reservation fees or cancellation fees is real cash but is not durable demand, and should be stripped out of the base before any coverage ratio is computed.

Third: read the transcripts adversarially. Search across quarters for the specific language — non-cancelable, take-or-pay, prepayment, committed capacity, sold out, minimum volume — and track how management's characterization drifts. Definitional drift in what counts as backlog or agreement value, introduced quietly in a quarter where the number would otherwise have fallen, is the highest-value tell in this entire dataset. Reconstruct every series on a consistent definition and footnote every restatement.

## Pitfalls I am explicitly watching

Double-ordering is the central one. In shortage conditions customers place orders at multiple suppliers for the same end demand, and the aggregate industry backlog exceeds any plausible end market. The check is arithmetic, not judgmental: sum committed volumes across the supplier set for the constrained product and compare to independent end-market unit forecasts. If the sum exceeds plausible demand by a wide margin, the coverage of every individual supplier is overstated by roughly that ratio, and mine is too.

Enforcement is the second. Suppliers rarely sue their largest customers. An agreement's legal terms describe the worst case; commercial reality is that relief gets granted to preserve the relationship. I treat contractual damages as an upper bound on recovery and assume the practical outcome is renegotiation, unless customer cash has already been received — which is why cash support dominates every other attribute in the tiering.

Third, reflexivity. The shortage guidance is itself a commercial tool designed to induce exactly the agreement-signing behavior this analysis measures. That means the correlation between shortage guidance and agreement announcements is close to mechanical and carries almost no information. Only the structural quality of the resulting agreements, and their eventual conversion, carries information. I will not let announcement count enter the conclusion.

Fourth, duration and level confusion. Long-dated coverage at flat volumes is durability without growth. If the market is paying a growth multiple, converting a cyclical into a stable-but-flat business is a valuation downgrade, not an upgrade. State that outcome explicitly if the numbers point to it.

## Decision rules and sequencing

Week one: build the RPO and backlog roll-forward on a consistent definition back through the prior cycle, and construct the agreement register with all six structural attributes populated. Week two: calibrate haircuts from the prior episode's realized conversion, run the customer-side mirror reconciliation, and compute segmented coverage. Week three: the three resolution scenarios with pricing, inventory-unwind and depreciation paths, then the two valuation comparisons and the historical base-rate backtest.

I commit to these thresholds in advance. Constructive requires all of: at least 60 percent of next-twelve-month revenue covered by Tier 1 and Tier 2 commitments with a stated ship schedule; at least 40 percent coverage in year two; customer cash support of at least 15 percent of committed value, or take-or-pay damages above 20 percent of the shortfall; top-three customer share of the agreement book below 45 percent; customer-side mirror disclosures reconciling to at least half the claimed value; and a modeled durable horizon exceeding the market-implied visibility horizon by two years or more. Negative on any of: implied gross bookings negative for two consecutive quarters; channel days of inventory above 1.5 times normal; a backlog or agreement-value definition changed in a quarter it would otherwise have declined; or supplier finished-goods inventory rising for two quarters while backlog is described as record.

The honest expected answer, which I will state plainly if the data supports it, is that shortage guidance reliably portends more announced agreements and materially less additional durable revenue than the announcements imply — and that the gap between those two is where the mispricing, in either direction, actually lives.
