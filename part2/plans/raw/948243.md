## The question, and the question behind it

The stated question is whether round-lot orders identify retail flow that is profitable to trade against. That is two claims stacked on top of each other, and the second one is worthless if the first is false. Claim one: a print of 100 shares (or a multiple of 100) carries information about whether the originating order came from a retail brokerage customer. Claim two: conditional on having isolated retail flow, taking the other side of it earns money after costs.

Almost every version of this study that has been run in a trading seat runs the two claims together — build a round-lot imbalance signal, backtest it, and if the backtest looks good, declare that round lots are retail. That is backwards. The signal can look good for reasons that have nothing to do with retail identification (round-lot imbalance is correlated with order flow imbalance generally, which is mechanically predictive at short horizons), and the retail story then gets attached as a post-hoc rationalization that will not survive the first regime change. So the entire first phase of this project tests only claim one, and the project has a hard stop before any signal is constructed.

**Hypothesis (H1):** Among trades in a given venue class, round-lot size predicts retail origination with enough precision and recall to be a usable filter — specifically, at least a 2x lift over the unconditional retail share of volume, capturing at least a third of retail volume, with directional signing accuracy well above chance.

**Null (H0):** Round-lot size is uninformative or weakly informative about retail origination once venue is controlled for, because institutional execution algorithms slice parent orders into 100-share children at massive scale while retail orders are increasingly notional-denominated ("buy $500 of NVDA") and therefore arrive as odd lots and mixed lots.

I will state my prior openly, because it determines how the tests are designed: I expect H0. The mechanism is specific. Retail brokerages have moved to dollar-denominated and fractional order entry; a $500 order in a $180 stock is 2.78 shares. Meanwhile, the modal child-order size from a VWAP/TWAP scheduler on a lit book is exactly 100 shares, and has been for fifteen years. If that is right, round lots are closer to an *institutional* signature than a retail one, and the correct deliverable from this project is a well-evidenced negative result plus a map of what actually does identify retail. That is a useful deliverable. The failure mode to avoid is spending four weeks building a signal on a proxy that was dead on arrival.

## What "retail" is benchmarked against

There is no public dataset that labels retail at the individual print level off-exchange. So the validation uses three imperfect labels and triangulates.

**Benchmark A — sub-penny price improvement on off-exchange prints.** The standard academic construction: take consolidated tape trades reported through a FINRA TRF (exchange code `D` in TAQ), exclude non-standard sale conditions (derivatively priced, average price, next-day, form-T outside regular hours, odd-lot-only condition codes), and compute the sub-penny remainder *z* = 100·P − floor(100·P). Trades with 0 < z < 0.4 are retail marketable sells (the wholesaler improved the bid downward by a fraction of a cent); 0.6 < z < 1 are retail marketable buys; z = 0 and z ≈ 0.5 are unclassified. Two known weaknesses must be handled explicitly rather than waved at. First, the signing is materially wrong — published re-examinations against actual broker records put sign-error rates in the tens of percent, because some wholesalers improve by a fixed increment regardless of side and some report the improvement on the far side. Second, and more damaging for a 2026 study, the migration of retail executions to midpoint pricing pushes real retail flow into the unclassified bucket, and the half-penny quoting increment for tick-constrained names under the amended minimum-pricing-increment rule mechanically breaks the sub-penny remainder logic. Benchmark A is therefore high-precision and low-recall, and it is only valid over a sample period ending before the half-penny quoting compliance date. That period restriction is a design constraint, not a footnote.

**Benchmark B — exchange retail liquidity program flags.** NYSE, NYSE American, NYSE Arca, Cboe (retail priority on EDGX/BYX), Nasdaq, MEMX and IEX all run programs where an order must be certified as originating from a natural person, entered by a designated Retail Member Organization, to receive price improvement or priority. Executions against these are identifiable on the exchanges' proprietary integrated/depth feeds and, for some, via sale condition on the tape. This is the closest thing to a gold standard: precision near 1.0 by construction (the exchange polices RMO certification), recall very low (RLP is a small fraction of retail volume). Day one task for the analyst: pull the current feed specifications for NYSE Pillar Integrated, Cboe PITCH, Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH and IEX DEEP and confirm, field by field, which ones carry an execution-level retail indicator in *historical* data, not just live. Do not assume; the specs change and vendor-normalized products often drop the flag. If only NYSE and IEX survive, that is enough for the tests below.

**Benchmark C — wholesaler accounting via Rule 605.** Monthly execution-quality reports from the six retail wholesalers (Citadel Securities, Virtu, G1X, Two Sigma Securities, Jane Street, UBS) cover essentially pure retail marketable flow. Under the amended rule these are broken out by size including odd lots and fractional shares, and by dollar-denominated buckets. This gives an aggregate, no-modeling-required answer to the core question: what fraction of wholesaler-executed retail volume is round-lot, and what fraction of all off-exchange round-lot volume in TAQ is attributable to wholesalers? Pair with FINRA's OTC transparency data (weekly shares and trades by MPID, ATS and non-ATS) to get average trade size per wholesaler MPID and to size the wholesaler share of the off-exchange tape. This test is cheap — a few days of spreadsheet work against public filings — and should be run *first*, before any tick data is touched, because it can kill the project on its own.

**Optional benchmark D — a commercial retail-flow dataset.** VandaTrack publishes daily per-ticker net retail buying derived from actual retail brokerage order flow. It is a genuinely external label with a different error structure from A, B and C. Budget roughly the cost of a mid-tier data subscription; if procurement is slow, the study proceeds on A/B/C and adds D as confirmation.

## Data and sample

Universe: CRSP share codes 10 and 11, primary listing NYSE/Nasdaq/NYSE American, price between $3 and $600, at least 250,000 shares median daily volume. Roughly 2,000–2,500 names, stratified into size quintiles and a separate high-price bucket ($250+) that will be used for a specific test below.

Sample period: 36 months ending at the last month fully before the half-penny quoting compliance date, so that Benchmark A remains interpretable throughout. Flag and separately analyze the tiered round-lot definition change from the market data infrastructure rule (100 shares under $250, 40 shares $250–$1,000, 10 shares $1,000–$10,000, 1 share above) — this creates a structural break in what "round lot" even means, and every construct must be computed under both the fixed-100 and the tiered definitions.

Core data: NYSE Daily TAQ trades and the TAQ NBBO file, nanosecond timestamps, full sale condition and exchange participant fields. Roughly 80–120 GB compressed per year. Land it as partitioned Parquet, one file per symbol-day, query with DuckDB locally and Spark for the full-panel passes. Add the exchange proprietary feeds identified above for the RLP flags, CRSP for returns/shares outstanding/delisting, and Compustat only if the eventual signal stage needs controls.

Quote handling: sign lit trades with Lee-Ready against the prevailing NBBO with a one-millisecond lag applied to the quote series, and report robustness to zero-lag and to the tick test. Never sign a trade against a quote stamped at or after the trade time.

## Phase 1 — partition, then validate

**Step 1: partition by venue before anything else.** Off-exchange round lots and lit round lots are different populations with different generating processes and must never be pooled. Split into (a) TRF/off-exchange prints, (b) lit prints on exchanges running an RLP, (c) lit prints on exchanges without one. Within off-exchange, further split by whether the print sits at, inside, or outside the NBB/NBO, and whether it carries sub-penny improvement, since that separates wholesaler internalization from ATS block crossing. Within each partition, decompose round lots into exactly-100, multiples of 100 above 100, and mixed lots (e.g. 137 shares) — the exactly-100 bucket is where scheduler child orders concentrate, and it is very likely the *least* retail bucket, which is diagnostic in itself.

**Step 2: the five gate tests.**

*G1 — Precision / lift.* Estimate P(retail | round lot, venue). Compute two ways: the Rule 605 accounting route (wholesaler round-lot volume divided by total off-exchange round-lot volume) and a latent-class estimate. The latent-class step is worth the effort: with three imperfect classifiers (Benchmark A's sub-penny label, Benchmark B's RLP flag where available, and the round-lot label itself) applied to the same population, a Hui–Walter-style two-population latent class model identifies each test's sensitivity and specificity without a gold standard. It requires conditional independence of errors given true class, which is violated here — the sub-penny label and venue are entangled — so estimate it within venue strata where the assumption is least offensive and report it as a bound, not a point estimate. **Kill threshold: proceed only if the lift over the unconditional retail share of volume in that venue is ≥ 2.0x with a bootstrap 95% lower bound ≥ 1.5x.** A lift below 1.5x means round lots are barely more retail than a random print.

*G2 — Recall.* What fraction of Benchmark A-classified and Benchmark B-flagged retail volume is round-lot? **Kill threshold: ≥ 35%.** Below that, even a perfectly precise filter is looking at a sliver of the population and any signal built on it is capacity-constrained and noisy.

*G3 — Signing accuracy.* Take RLP-flagged executions where true side is known and apply the signing rule the round-lot signal would use. **Kill threshold: ≥ 70% agreement; hard stop below 60%.** An imbalance signal built on a 60%-accurate signer has most of its variance coming from classification noise.

*G4 — Aggregate agreement.* At stock-day level, correlate round-lot order imbalance with (i) Benchmark A imbalance and (ii) the commercial retail flow series, cross-sectionally and in the time series per stock, with Fisher-z confidence intervals. **Kill thresholds: mean correlation ≥ 0.40 against A and ≥ 0.25 against D.**

*G5 — Incremental information.* Panel regression of forward returns (1, 5, 20 days) on Benchmark A imbalance plus round-lot imbalance, standard errors double-clustered by stock and date. If round-lot imbalance carries no independent coefficient (|t| < 3), the round-lot construct is a degraded copy of a proxy we already have and adds nothing even if G1–G4 pass.

**Step 3: two mechanism tests that are cheap and decisive.** First, the price-elasticity test. If retail orders are notional-denominated, the round-lot share of known-retail flow should fall sharply with share price, while the round-lot share of institutional flow should not (schedulers still use 100-share children). Estimate the elasticity of round-lot frequency with respect to log price separately for RLP-flagged prints and for all prints; if the RLP-flagged elasticity is strongly negative and the all-prints elasticity is flat, round lots are picking up the non-retail population. Second, the round-notional test: compute the distribution of trade *notional* and look for clustering at $100/$500/$1,000/$5,000 among RLP-flagged prints versus round-share clustering among lit prints. Whichever clustering dominates in the round-lot bucket tells you who is generating it.

**Gate rule:** proceed to Phase 2 only if G1, G2, G3 and G4 all pass within at least one venue partition *and* G5 passes within that same partition. Any other outcome, including "passes off-exchange but fails on signing," terminates the project. The write-up in that case is the deliverable: which partitions failed, by how much, the estimated sensitivity/specificity of each retail proxy, and a recommendation on what to use instead.

## Phase 2 — only if the gate passes

Build retail order imbalance from round-lot prints within the surviving partition, at 30-minute and daily frequency, as (buy − sell) / (buy + sell) volume, winsorized at 1%. Test both directions of the trade, and be disciplined about which economic claim is being tested, because "trade against retail" conflates two different businesses.

*Claim (i), the directional signal.* Decile sorts on lagged imbalance, equal- and value-weighted, held 1/5/20 days, with Newey-West t-stats and a Fama-MacBeth cross-check controlling for size, book-to-market, momentum, short-term reversal, illiquidity and the prior day's total order imbalance — that last control is essential, since round-lot imbalance is mechanically correlated with total imbalance and the short-horizon reversal it produces. Note that the published finding for retail imbalance at weekly horizons is *positive* continuation, meaning trading *with* retail, not against; if the result comes out negative here, treat that as evidence of a construct problem rather than a discovery until it is reproduced in Benchmark A imbalance too.

*Claim (ii), the liquidity-provision business.* This is what "profitable to trade against" usually means in practice: retail marketable flow is uninformed, so supplying liquidity to it earns the spread without adverse selection. Test it directly with a spread decomposition — effective spread, five-minute and thirty-minute realized spread, and price impact — computed for round-lot prints versus non-round-lot prints within each venue partition. If round lots genuinely carry retail, their realized spread should be markedly higher and their permanent impact markedly lower. This test is worth running even in a partial-failure scenario, because it is informative about the *economics* independent of the labeling.

Costs and capacity: charge two times the contemporaneous effective half-spread plus 5 bps of fees/impact, cap participation at 3% of ADV per name per day, and report gross-to-net decay. **Go/no-go: after-cost Sharpe ≥ 1.0 over the full sample with no single calendar year negative, and net-to-gross ratio above 0.40.**

## Pitfalls I would watch for

The meme-stock window will dominate any retail-flow panel that includes it; run everything with and without it and never let it drive a conclusion. Stock splits reshuffle round-lot economics for individual names — exclude a window around split dates. Odd lots were absent from the consolidated tape before 2013, so any extension of the sample backwards silently changes the denominator of every test. The tiered round-lot definition and the half-penny quoting increment are both structural breaks that hit the round-lot construct and the sub-penny benchmark respectively; treat them as sample boundaries, not as controls. The benchmarks are noisy labels, so naive agreement statistics understate true agreement — this is exactly why the latent-class step exists, and why the kill thresholds are set on lift rather than raw precision. Multiple testing is a live risk: three venue partitions times three benchmarks times three horizons is enough surface area to find something; pre-register the gate thresholds in writing before the first regression and apply Benjamini-Hochberg to the Phase 2 results. Fractional-share executions get aggregated and reported in ways that vary by wholesaler, which distorts the odd-lot denominator. Finally, "retail" itself is drifting — a growing share of what arrives through retail brokerage pipes is systematic day-trading, not uninformed household flow, which erodes the entire economic premise even if the identification works.
