# 0be96c

## The question behind the question

Management has told us supply is short through next year and, explicitly or by implication, that customers are responding by locking in volume under long-term agreements. The market reads that as a duration story: revenue that used to be a spot book is now a contracted book, so the multiple should go up. The claim has two joints, and both can fail. First, that customers will actually sign binding commitments rather than non-binding reservations. Second — the one that matters more and gets tested less — that those commitments survive contact with normalized supply. Every shortage ends. The interesting question is what the paper is worth on the day it stops being scarce.

## Hypothesis

My prior, to be falsified: shortage-era long-term agreements are, in the median case, honored in volume and broken in price. They function as capacity insurance for the customer and as a customer-retention device for the supplier, not as a floor under revenue. The distribution of outcomes is bimodal and the splitter is not contract language — it is switching cost. Where the customer cannot requalify a second source inside the contract term (aerospace forgings, auto-grade power semis mid-program, qualified HBM stacks tied to a specific accelerator SKU), agreements hold at something close to stated terms. Where requalification is cheap or the product is fungible (commodity polysilicon, standard logic at mature nodes, container slots, generic APIs), agreements get repriced to spot within two to four quarters of the supply overhang landing, regardless of what the take-or-pay clause says, because suppliers will not sue their largest customers.

Secondary hypothesis: the announcement of a large LTA book is a coincident indicator of cycle peak, not a leading indicator of durability. The prepayments that fund the LTA book fund the capacity that destroys the pricing.

The falsifying evidence would be a set of prior episodes where contracted price realization stayed materially above spot for eight or more quarters after supply normalized, across suppliers without a hard qualification moat. If I find that, the durability claim is real and I should pay for it.

## The episodes I would assemble

I want roughly twenty to thirty supplier-side episodes with observable outcomes, deliberately spanning industries so the result is not an artifact of one market's customs. The starting list, all of which have public paper trails on both sides:

Polysilicon, 2007–2012. The cleanest natural experiment we have. Hemlock, Wacker, OCI, MEMC and REC signed multi-year take-or-pay contracts at $60–100/kg with prepayments, against a spot that had spiked toward $400 and then collapsed below $25. Outcomes: mass renegotiation, several outright walks, and litigation that resolved years later — Hemlock's judgments against SolarWorld and its arbitrations against Chinese buyers, MEMC's terminations with Suntech and Conergy, REC's restructurings. This episode gives me the enforcement base rate and, critically, the recovery-timing distribution on a "won" take-or-pay.

Automotive and industrial semiconductors, 2021–2024. Infineon, NXP, STMicro, onsemi and Renesas converted allocation into non-cancellable/non-returnable order books and multi-year LTSAs, onsemi's disclosed at roughly $16bn of committed volume. Wolfspeed took a ~$2bn deposit from Renesas against SiC wafer supply. By 2023–2025 the industrial and auto inventory correction hit; watch what happened to LTSA volumes, to the deposit, and to the language in each company's disclosure as it migrated from "committed" to "flexible."

Memory, twice: DRAM/NAND 2017–2019 and HBM 2023–2026. The first gives a completed cycle with contract-versus-spot price series from TrendForce; the second is the live analogue and the holdout.

MLCC and passives, 2017–2019. Murata and Samsung Electro-Mechanics, sold out, customers begging for allocation, then a 2019 price collapse. Small-ticket, highly fungible, no qualification moat — my expected worst case for durability.

ABF substrates and packaging, 2021–2023 (Ibiden, Unimicron, Nan Ya), where customers made prepayments toward dedicated capacity.

Uranium enrichment and concentrate, 2007–2013. Utility LTAs signed into the 2007 spike, then Fukushima; watch deferrals, flex-down provisions and market-related pricing with floors, which is the mechanism that let those contracts survive in form while collapsing in economics.

LNG SPAs, 2008 and 2012 vintages. Twenty-year take-or-pay with destination restrictions — the highest-quality LTAs in industry — and yet routinely reopened through contractual price-review clauses. This is my upper bound on durability, and it still shows economic erosion.

Ocean container contracts, 2021–2023. Annual and multi-year contracts at $6,000–8,000 per FEU that were abandoned by shippers within months of spot breaking, with essentially no enforcement.

Lithium and cobalt offtakes, 2017–2020 and 2022–2024. Fixed-price offtakes signed at the peak that converted to index-linked with lags, plus the Livent/Tesla and SQM/Albemarle structures.

Titanium and aero forgings, and aftermarket aerostructure LTAs generally. My expected best case, and a useful control: these are called LTAs too, and they hold, which tells me the label is not the variable.

For each supplier episode I want the matching customer, because the single most powerful test in this plan is reading the same contract from both sides.

## Data required

Supplier side: 10-K/20-F/annual reports for the shortage year and each of the four following years; Exhibit 10 material contracts, which even redacted usually expose the deficiency-payment formula, the make-up rights and the term; ASC 606 remaining performance obligation disclosures with the practical-expedient carve-outs stated; contract liability balances and their roll-forward; any onerous-contract or deposit-forfeiture entries; and the full transcript history, which is where LTA language quietly mutates.

Customer side: the contractual obligations and purchase commitments tables in Item 7 and the commitments footnote. If a supplier claims $16bn of committed LTA volume, the customers' purchase-obligation tables should show it. In prior episodes they largely do not, and the gap is the measurement I want.

Third-party price series to separate price from volume: TrendForce/DRAMeXchange contract versus spot for memory, Fastmarkets and Benchmark Mineral for lithium, UxC for uranium, Argus and ICIS for polysilicon and chemicals, Drewry and Xeneta for container contract-versus-spot, and shipbroker COA data for bulk.

Physical shipment verification: bill-of-lading and customs data (Panjiva, ImportGenius, Korean and Taiwanese customs aggregates) at the specific supplier-customer pair level. This is how I check whether contracted volume actually moved, independent of what either party said.

Legal: PACER, Delaware Chancery, and where visible, ICC/SIAC arbitration disclosures, plus Korean, Taiwanese, German and Japanese filings for the non-US counterparties. Litigation is a censored sample — most breaches never get sued — but the cases that exist reveal the terms verbatim.

Capacity: SEMI's fab database, company capex guidance and equipment-order disclosures, plant-level announcements with construction start dates, and for each, the qualification lag appropriate to the end market (automotive PPAP and AEC-Q qualification runs twelve to twenty-four months after tool-in; consumer runs three to six).

## How I would run it

Step one, define the event precisely so the sample isn't assembled by hindsight. An episode enters when the supplier first uses allocation language in a public filing or call — "sold out through," "on allocation," "lead times extended to X weeks" — and I take that quarter as T=0 using the transcript date, not with knowledge of what followed. I build the list from a full-text search of that language across EDGAR and foreign equivalents, then keep every hit meeting a size threshold, including the ones that turned out boring. Selecting episodes from memory of famous blowups would guarantee the answer.

Step two, code the contracts, not the headlines. For each LTA I record: is it take-or-pay with liquidated damages, or a capacity reservation with a fee; if take-or-pay, is there a make-up right that lets shortfall payments be credited against future purchases — this is the clause that converts "revenue" into a deferred liability and it is present far more often than the press release suggests; is the prepayment refundable, non-refundable, or creditable, and against what; pricing as fixed, indexed, collar, or cost-pass-through with a lag; presence of a meet-competition or most-favored-customer clause, which is a self-destruct mechanism once a rival's new plant is bidding; volume flex bands and whether they are annual or cumulative; price-review or reopener rights and their trigger; termination-for-convenience with the penalty schedule; change-of-control and assignment; force majeure scope; and the counterparty's credit standing at signature, because an unsecured claim against a distressed customer is worth cents.

Step three, code the outcome on an ordinal scale at T+4, T+8 and T+12 quarters: fully honored at contract price; honored in volume, repriced down; deferred with term extension; converted into prepayment credits or offset against future purchases; terminated with a negotiated payment; walked with no payment; litigated. I measure realized revenue against LTA-implied revenue, realized price against the relevant spot index, and gross margin path, and I separate the price and volume contributions explicitly so a contract that "held" on tonnage at half the price doesn't get scored as durable.

Step four, produce the base rate and the conditional base rates. Unconditionally: what share of shortage-era LTA value was realized at contracted economics through maturity. Then conditionally on the features that plausibly matter — qualification lag, single-versus-dual source, customer credit, fixed-versus-indexed pricing, presence of make-up rights, whether the end demand behind the customer was itself contracted. With twenty to thirty episodes I will not be running regressions; I will rank-order features by the outcome distribution within each group and be honest that the evidence is ordinal.

Step five, the capacity ledger, because the base rate only becomes actionable when I know the date the overhang lands. For the subject company's market I build a plant-by-plant addition schedule: announced capacity, greenfield versus debottleneck, construction start, tool-in, qualification lag by end market, and a ramp curve to effective output rather than nameplate. I also add capacity that arrives without construction — yield improvement, node migration freeing wafer starts, dies reclaimed from a decaying adjacent market, and Chinese domestic supply, which historically arrives later than announced and then all at once. Against that I place the weighted-average maturity of the LTA book and the maturity of the specific price terms, which often reset earlier than the contract expires. The single number I want out of this is the gap in quarters between effective supply-demand balance and weighted-average LTA maturity. Where prior episodes had a positive gap of four quarters or more, renegotiation was the norm; where the contract matured before the capacity landed, the LTA never had to be tested and was quietly renewed at a lower price.

Step six, the customer's breach arithmetic, done explicitly. For each maturity year, compute the customer's savings from buying at my forecast spot instead of contract, against the present value of the penalty plus the expected cost of the relationship damage. When savings exceed the penalty by a wide margin, assume renegotiation regardless of language, because history says the supplier will offer it before the customer has to ask. That asymmetry — suppliers preemptively conceding to preserve share — is why the enforcement base rate is so low, and it needs to be in the model as a behavioral input, not a legal one.

## Validation

I hold out one completed episode entirely — my preference is the 2021–2024 auto semiconductor cycle, because it is recent, large and well-documented — build the framework on everything else, and then predict the holdout's outcomes before looking. I write down the predictions with dates and thresholds first.

For the live case, I pre-register three to five falsifiable checks with deadlines: the contract liability balance moves in a stated direction by a stated quarter; RPO grows by at least X% of the announced LTA value within two quarters of signature, or else the agreements are not binding in the accounting sense; customs data shows shipment volumes to the named counterparties tracking the contracted schedule within a band; the customer's next 10-K purchase-obligation table rises by an amount consistent with the supplier's claim. Each of these is checkable within twelve months and each can prove me wrong.

Cross-validation on measurement: reconcile every supplier claim against the counterparty's disclosure and against physical shipment data. Where the three disagree, the shipment data wins.

I also test the framework against its own best case. If aerospace and LNG LTAs score as durable and MLCC and container contracts score as fragile, and the ordering is predicted by qualification lag rather than by contract type, the framework is doing real work. If everything scores the same, I have built a tautology.

## Pitfalls I am watching for

Definitional drift is the biggest one. "LTA" covers binding take-or-pay, non-binding capacity reservations, framework agreements with volumes "to be agreed annually," and pure design wins. Companies exploit the ambiguity in exactly the quarter the market wants duration. Every episode gets classified from primary contract text or the accounting treatment, never from the headline.

Disclosure asymmetry. Signature is announced; unwind is silent. A study built only on disclosed outcomes will overstate durability badly. Customer filings, customs data and court dockets exist precisely to fill that hole.

Double counting between backlog, RPO and stated LTA value — the same volume gets presented three ways and summed by the sell side.

Prepayment as disguised financing. A customer prepayment that funds the supplier's capex is a loan with equity-like risk, and its accounting flatters near-term cash while pre-committing future revenue at a credited (often below-market) price. The recycling is self-defeating: the prepayment builds the capacity that ends the shortage.

Index pass-through masking real price cuts. A contract that "held" while its reference index fell 60% did not hold economically.

Confusing a demand-driven shortage inside secular growth with a supply-shock shortage inside a flat market. The former produces LTAs that get honored because the customer still needs the volume; the latter produces LTAs that get repriced. This distinction likely explains more outcome variance than anything in the contract.

Survivorship in the sample: suppliers that failed outright stop filing, and their LTAs vanish from the record, biasing toward durability.

Finally, the possibility that I am right about the base rate and wrong about this case. The output should not be a verdict but a haircut: a probability-weighted realization schedule on the stated LTA book, with an explicit statement of what would have to be true — a qualification barrier longer than the capacity gap, a customer whose own revenue is contracted, non-creditable prepayments, no meet-competition clause — for the durability claim to deserve a higher multiple.
