# S9 — critique (claude-code subagent)

> A company just guided to product shortages lasting through next year. Does that portend more customer long-term agreements and more durable revenue?

## Verdict

This is a well-read plan written by someone who has clearly watched the 2021–23 semiconductor cycle and knows where the bodies are. The pitfalls section is better than most published papers. The honest null ("shortage + LTA wave is a late-cycle sell signal") is the right null and I believe it.

But the plan does not survive its own data. Two of the three primary outcome variables — the ASC 606 contract-liability tag and remaining performance obligation — do not exist before 2018. The plan's own kill criterion requires 120+ events outside 2020–2022. Those two facts cannot both be satisfied. The study as designed is a 2018–2023 study, which is to say it is the COVID episode, which is the thing the plan says would justify shutting it down. Nobody noticed the collision because ASC 606 is filed under "comparability pitfall" rather than "this deletes fifteen of my twenty-three years."

Separately, the six-week plan places its first go/no-go at end of week 3, after the entire hand-labeling budget has been spent, when the binding question — how many qualifying events actually exist — is answerable in two days.

My recommendation is not to run this. Run the case-study step that is already sitting at line 62 of the plan, alone, and let it decide whether anything else is worth building. Details at the end.

## The three that kill it

**1. The measurement window and the sample window are mutually exclusive.** Event tape: 2003–2026. Primary H1 outcome: `us-gaap:ContractWithCustomerLiabilityCurrent`/`Noncurrent`. Primary durability outcome: `us-gaap:RevenueRemainingPerformanceObligation`. Both are ASC 606 constructs, effective for calendar 2018. Before that you have `DeferredRevenueCurrent`, which is a different concept with different scope — and before ~2011 you have no XBRL at all for most of the filer population (phase-in ran 2009–2011, largest floats first, so the pre-2011 coverage hole is correlated with size, which is correlated with treatment). RPO simply does not exist pre-2018 in any form.

So the honest sample for the outcomes that carry the argument is 2018–2023 with a t+8 requirement, i.e. events through roughly 2023. That is one shortage episode. Every cycle-control, holdout and placebo in the Validation section is designed to defend against exactly the "one macro episode" failure — and the outcome data guarantees it. The plan's stop rule fires on day one, not in week three.

*What it would take:* pick outcomes that exist across the full span. Inventory days, days sales outstanding, customer deposits/advances hand-collected from balance sheets, gross margin, revenue path, backlog conversion — these run back to 2003. Contract liabilities and RPO can be secondary evidence on the post-2018 slice. If you insist on RPO as primary, then say up front that this is a study of 2018–2023 and drop the pretence of a 23-year panel, the 2003–2015 estimation window and the "excluding 2020Q2–2022Q4" robustness cut, all of which become empty.

**2. The event count is asserted, not estimated, and the estimate is generous.** "400–900 qualifying events" is a guess. The definition is demanding: an executive, in prepared remarks or Q&A, asserting supply cannot meet demand *and* attaching a horizon of four quarters or more, precision-tuned. Outside 2020–2022 the real inventory of such statements is thin — 2010–11 (post-crisis restock), 2017–18 (DRAM/NAND, MLCC, passives), scattered aerospace and auto-grade cases, some energy equipment. My prior is that the out-of-COVID count lands in the low hundreds at best on a loose classifier and well under 120 on a precision-tuned one. Which means the week-3 kill fires.

*What it would take:* two days, not three weeks. Run a crude keyword-and-horizon regex over whatever transcript corpus you already have — "sold out through", "allocation through", "constrained through", "shortage through" plus a year token — count hits by year, dedupe to firm-quarter, eyeball a hundred of them. That number decides whether there is a project. Do it before you buy a second coder's time, before the Compustat pull, before anything. It is also worth confirming on day one that your seat actually licenses point-in-time/unrestated Compustat quarterly — the standard Fundamentals Quarterly file is restated, and many seats do not carry the PIT snapshot the plan assumes.

**3. The treatment is a disclosure decision, not a shortage.** The plan knows this — the "negotiating instrument" pitfall is exactly it — but files it as a robustness test rather than as the thing that determines what the coefficient means. TI was capacity-constrained in 2021 and declined to guide to it; Microchip guided loudly and built a Preferred Supply Program on the back of it. The treated set is not "firms with shortages," it is "firms that chose to publicise shortages," and that choice is made by the same commercial strategy that produces the LTA book. Propensity matching on log assets, revenue growth, margin, capex, inventory days and leverage does not touch selection on a disclosure decision. Every H1 coefficient is then partly definitional: firms that talk up scarcity to drive LTAs sign more LTAs.

*What it would take:* invert the design. Define treatment on a physical constraint — inventory days falling to a firm-specific low, book-to-bill, lead-time data where it exists, capacity utilisation — and use shortage *language* as a moderator. That gives you "among firms that got constrained, does telling the market about it change the contracting outcome," which is a real and much more interesting question, and it is identified off something other than the press release. If the physical measures are too coarse to define treatment, that is itself a finding worth two days of work and it tells you this design cannot be rescued.

## Design breaks that need fixing regardless

**Not-yet-treated controls are contaminated in precisely the direction of the effect.** Callaway–Sant'Anna needs no-spillover. When a supplier goes on allocation, its customers buy from the nearest substitute — which, under "propensity-score match within four-digit GICS sub-industry," is the control group by construction. The control's revenue rises because the treated firm is constrained. You have built the matching rule to maximise the SUTVA violation, and the bias runs against finding a positive H2 effect (or, if the control also becomes constrained, it becomes treated and drops out, which selects the control pool on being *un*constrained — i.e. on losing share, which is worse). This is not mentioned anywhere in the plan. At minimum: estimate the spillover directly (do same-sub-industry non-guiders show revenue acceleration at t+1 to t+4?), and rename the estimand — what you can identify is a *relative within-industry* effect, not a level effect.

**The pre-trend gate has its logic backwards, and it is the first go/no-go.** "Require p > 0.10 on the joint lead test" rewards imprecision: with 150 clustered events and noisy quarterly revenue growth, you will fail to reject zero pre-trends whether or not they exist, and you will pass your own gate on the strength of wide standard errors. The 0.25-SD magnitude condition helps but is applied to point estimates, not intervals. Require the lead *confidence intervals* to exclude economically meaningful pre-trends. As written, the week-3 validation gate is close to non-binding — which is the failure mode where the study proceeds on a foundation nobody actually checked.

**No power calculation anywhere, and the design needs one badly.** The headline H2 estimand is a t+8 revenue-path coefficient on a firm-level outcome with enormous idiosyncratic variance, on a few hundred events whose effective independent count is far lower because they cluster into perhaps three or four macro cohorts, then cut five ways by pre-specified moderator, then Romano–Wolf corrected across the moderator family. The likely output is a fan of insignificant coefficients. That is not a null result — it is an uninterpretable one, because the study was never powered to distinguish "no effect" from "we could not see it." Compute the MDE in week 1 off historical revenue-growth dispersion. If it lands anywhere near 10–15pp of eight-quarter revenue growth, the moderator cuts are decoration and should be dropped from the pre-registration rather than reported as nulls.

**The holdout does not hold anything out for H2.** "Estimate on 2003–2015, treat 2016–2026 as holdout." Events after roughly 2023Q4 have no t+8, let alone t+12. Combined with the ASC 606 problem, the estimation window has no outcome data and the holdout window has no outcome horizon. For H2 there is exactly one usable slice and it is the one you were trying to guard against. Say this explicitly rather than reporting a holdout exercise that is mechanically empty.

**The attribution split is not identification.** "Supply-caused vs demand-caused" is coded from management's own words, and which story management tells is a communications choice about the same underlying fact. "We are capacity constrained" and "demand came in above plan" describe one world. Calling this "the closest thing we have to identification" overstates it by a lot. The genuinely exogenous variation is the hand-built shock list — Naka, AKM, Uri, Hualien, Ever Given, gallium/germanium controls. That list yields maybe eight to twelve episodes and a modest number of directly-affected firms. That is a case-study design. Promote it to primary and be honest that it is small-n, or drop the identification claim.

**The contract-liability tag probably misses the instrument you care about.** Contract liabilities blend ordinary deferred revenue — services, warranties, software, extended support — with the capacity deposits you actually want, and for a diversified hardware firm the ordinary component dominates the variance. Meanwhile the famous shortage-era prepayments frequently sit in customer deposits or other long-term liabilities under *firm-specific extension tags*, which the SEC DERA Financial Statement Data Sets will not surface under a standard `us-gaap` query. Extension-tag usage correlates with firm size and industry, so the hole is aligned with treatment. The "hardest to game" primary is the one most likely to systematically miss the thing.

**The backtest as specified is not implementable and its IR will be noise.** The long/short book is formed on "flagged durable by the moderator model" — a model fit on the full sample. An honest version re-fits the moderator classification using only information available at each rebalance date. With a few hundred events across 20+ years, clustered into a couple of episodes, most quarters have zero or one name on each side; sector- and beta-neutralising that is not possible. Either drop it, or replace it with an event-time cumulative abnormal return chart by moderator group and label it illustrative.

## Smaller, but real

- Six weeks, one analyst: 800 hand labels plus a second coder for kappa, hand-coding of hundreds of EX-10 exhibits for bindingness/prepayment/termination fee, twelve hand-traced case studies, CS + Sun–Abraham + BJS + local projections + honest-DiD + 1,000-draw placebo + backtest. Week 4 is "H1 estimation plus twelve case studies." This is a quarter of work for two people. The second coder is nowhere in the staffing.
- The second coder is a hard dependency on the week-2 deliverable and no one is named.
- Classifier error is not stationary. Shortage vocabulary in 2004 transcripts differs from 2022 ("allocation," "sold out through," "de-commit" are era-specific). An LLM tuned for precision will have era- and sector-varying recall, which is differential measurement error correlated with the cohort structure — the one thing the design cannot absorb.
- `ORDBKLG` is an annual Compustat item, not quarterly. Fine as background colour, useless for a quarterly event window.
- Price-vs-volume decomposition "wherever unit data exists": for US-listed non-financials, unit or ASP data essentially never exists outside autos and a few memory names. This step will silently not happen; plan around its absence rather than counting on it.
- The market-outcome block (CAR plus 12/24-month DGTW) is attached to no hypothesis and is not in the Romano–Wolf family. Either give it a hypothesis or cut it.
- H1 is conceded to be "close to a truism" and consumes weeks 2 and 4. It is also partly mechanical: a constrained firm taking cash it cannot yet ship against will book higher contract liabilities by accounting identity. The truism will be confirmed, expensively.

## What is right

Worth saying plainly, because it is unusual. The two-claim decomposition is correct and most people would have run the truism and stopped. The refusal to use naive TWFE with staggered treatment is correct. Insisting on gross margin alongside revenue durability — because prepaid capacity repaid in product at fixed prices persists without creating value — is the single sharpest observation in the document and most sell-side work on this topic misses it. Backlog *conversion* rather than backlog level, given double-ordering, is right. "Enforceability is social, not legal" is right and is the reason the case studies matter more than the panel. Pre-registering the primary outcome and stating the kill criteria in advance is right. The willingness to publish the negative finding is the best thing here.

## The cheaper version

The plan already contains it, at line 62, buried in Validation and scheduled for week 6.

Take 20–25 firms with large disclosed LTA/capacity-commitment books built during 2020–2022 — the semi and power-semi names, the auto-grade suppliers, a couple of aerospace and industrial cases — plus a deliberate abstainer control group (TI is the obvious one; find three or four more). For each, trace by hand: announced contract face value at signing → subsequent RPO and contract-liability disclosure → revenue actually recognised through 2024–25 → any renegotiation, de-commit, deferral or quiet disappearance in the 10-K language. Compute a realisation ratio per firm. Compare the revenue drawdown depth and gross-margin path of committers versus abstainers through the downcycle.

That is one to two weeks for one analyst using filings and transcripts you can get without a Capital IQ seat. It answers the question that carries money — does contractual lock-in produce durable revenue — with data that actually exists, over the only episode with enough cases to look at. If realisation ratios come in near 1.0 and committers held up better than abstainers, you have a surprising result and a reason to build the panel. If they come in at 0.5 with a trail of renegotiations, you have the answer, it took two weeks, and the panel would only have added false precision on top of it.

If someone insists on the panel anyway, the minimum changes are: size the event tape in week 1 before anything else; move the outcome set to variables that exist pre-2018; define treatment on a physical constraint with language as moderator; fix the control pool so it is not the treated firms' direct substitutes; compute the MDE and cut the moderator cuts it cannot support; and drop the backtest.
